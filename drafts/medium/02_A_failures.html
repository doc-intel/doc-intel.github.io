When RAG Embeddings Return the Wrong Chunk (And Why a Bigger Model Won't Fix It)

What vector search actually handles, where it silently returns the wrong chunk even with the best OpenAI or open-source model, and the one mechanism that explains both the wins and the failures

The standard advice for better RAG retrieval is to reach for a stronger embedding model: swap ada-002 for 3-large, or a small local encoder for a hosted flagship, and the numbers follow. That advice is half right. Embeddings do handle a lot. Ask "how do I cancel" on a document that only ever says termination and the right line comes back. Ask in French against an English document, still the right line. Add a typo, it survives. No synonym table, no rules, nothing hand-coded.

Then the same system silently returns the wrong chunk, and no change of provider fixes it. Ask "what is NOT covered by this policy" and cosine similarity returns everything that IS covered, because the word not barely moves the vector. Ask for a product code the model never saw at training and it lands on a chunk about a different tier. Ask about non-employee labor when the document says contractors and you get nothing: same concept, wrong term, wrong chunk.

None of these are edge cases. They are how cosine similarity is built. What breaks in one model breaks in the next with a slightly different score. Replacing OpenAI with a better model, a bigger model, or a local model does not fix the failure mode. It just moves the failure line.

This article catalogues what embeddings actually handle and where they return the wrong chunk, side by side on four models: a cheap one (GloVe), a small local sentence encoder (MiniLM), and two hosted flagships (OpenAI ada-002 and 3-large). Section 1 shows the wins (conceptual proximity, synonyms, typos, cross-lingual matching). Section 2 walks eight failure modes one at a time (from the out-of-vocabulary break to negation, magnitudes, and long-context dilution), then repeats them page by page on a real PDF. Section 3 names the single mechanism behind both: embeddings rank text by how tokens co-occurred in the model's training corpus, not by whether one text answers the question the other one asks. That is also why the failures hit enterprise vocabulary harder than public-web content.

One term to fix before we start. Cosine similarity is the score embeddings use: a number between 0 and 1 that measures how closely two vectors point in the same direction. A score of 1 means identical direction, 0 means unrelated. You'll see it throughout the article.

1. What embeddings handle

Before the failures, the wins. The failures only make sense in contrast.

An embedding turns a piece of text into a vector. Texts with similar terms are close in vector space. The model has seen "phone number" and "telephone" used in similar contexts millions of times, so their vectors land near each other. Same for "premium" and "monthly cost", or "policy" and "polciy" (the typo).

Every case below runs on the same four models: a cheap dense one (GloVe, 300 dims, 2014), a small sentence encoder (MiniLM, 384 dims, 2020), and two production-grade OpenAI models (ada-002 3072 dims and 3-large 3072 dims, 2022/2024).

1.1 Conceptual proximity

Query insurance. Every model ranks the insurance-topical paraphrase far above an unrelated sentence

The topical paraphrase wins by a wide margin on every model, from GloVe's 2014 vectors to OpenAI's 2024 3-large. The paraphrase never uses the word insurance and yet lands close to it in vector space. That is the ability that makes embeddings useful.

1.2 Synonyms and paraphrase

The premium synonym wins across models, ahead of an office-hours control that shares no policy vocabulary

Same result on synonyms. premium never appears in the query; policy never appears in the winning candidate. The bridge is the shared meaning.

1.3 Typos and misspellings

A double typo query still lands on the correct target, thanks to subword tokenisation

Modern models tokenise below the word level. A typo like polciy shares most of its subword pieces with policy, so the vectors stay close. GloVe misses (it works at the whole-word level; typos land outside its vocabulary), MiniLM and the two OpenAI models recover cleanly.

1.4 Cross-lingual matching

A French query retrieves the English target line, with no manual translation step

Multilingual embeddings pull the right cross-language match, so a French user query lands on the English line that answers it. The ability disappears with English-only models (GloVe on English Wikipedia knows no French, so it fails).

1.5 When one word means two things: compound polysemy

Compound polysemy: the winning candidate is the correct sense, but decoys pull the margins down

A word like hot dog (the food) shares tokens with hot (temperature) and dog (animal). The model handles this with limited grace: the correct compound sense usually wins, but the margin over decoys is thinner than for a clean paraphrase.

The green-card / green-jersey / immigrant example makes this even more concrete: the compound term wins, but the drop to the second-ranked decoy is much smaller than in 1.1.

Same shape as hot dog: the correct sense wins on the OpenAI models, but decoys stay close

1.6 What these wins show, and don't

The wins above are real. They are the reason embeddings became the default recall step in every RAG pipeline built since 2021. They also share a common shape: the query and the target are both line-length, both on-topic, and the target contains a term the model already saw (a synonym, a paraphrase, or a shared token piece).

They do NOT show: that embeddings understand questions, that they can find the answer to a factoid, that they can compare magnitudes, that they know when to invert on negation. Those are different tasks. Sections 2.1 through 2.8 walk through them one at a time.

The obvious objection is "why not just pass the top-k to an LLM and let it read them?" That is what RAG systems actually do, of course. The question is whether the top-k contains the answer at all. When the top-k systematically excludes the answer-bearing line (sections 2.1-2.8 give the mechanics), no LLM downstream can rescue it. And bypassing retrieval entirely, feeding the whole corpus to the LLM on every query, only works while the corpus is small and cheap.

2. Where they break: eight failure modes

The abilities in section 1 are real; the failures below are equally real, equally reproducible, and persist across all four models. A larger model does not move the ranking. The fix is architectural, not "pick a stronger embedding".

Section 1.6 already raised the obvious counter ("for these cases, just ask the LLM directly"). That approach does not scale: a 200k-document corpus cannot be passed through an LLM on every query. Some retrieval step has to come first. The mainstream pipeline stacks a reranker between embeddings and the LLM; the series argues for upstream filtering through expert keywords and document structure (Articles 6, 7, 9). Either way, the failures below apply to the embedding stage. None of these layers is magic.

The eight failures group into three shapes.

  • Section 2.1 is the OOV break, where the term simply is not in the model.
  • Section 2.2 through 2.6 share one structural root: the embedding sees term similarity, not question-to-answer relevance, and each sub-section walks a specific query shape where this root causes the wrong chunk to win.
  • Section 2.7 collects the obvious cases (identifiers, boolean, counting, temporal, multi-hop) that need a different tool entirely.
  • Section 2.8 validates the whole set at page granularity on a real PDF.

2.1 The simplest break: the term isn't in the model

Before the structural failures, the most basic one. Section 1.6 said it in words. Here is the demo.

Take pool. In an insurance contract, pool is a risk-sharing instrument: a group of insureds that collectively absorb losses through aggregated premiums. In general English, pool is a body of water you swim in. Two senses of the same word, with one stark difference: the swimming sense is everywhere on the public web; the risk-pool sense is buried in actuarial textbooks, regulatory filings, and reinsurance treaties that the model barely saw at training time.

The test mirrors the hot-dog setup from section 1.5, with one twist. Query the bare word pool. Three candidates: a swimming paraphrase (the public sense, no pool token in the sentence), a reinsurance paraphrase using real industry jargon (the specialist sense, also no pool token), and a random control sentence about a train departure (no pool token, no insurance connection, no swimming).

Query pool. The reinsurance sense ranks below a random control on three of four models

The swim paraphrase wins on every model, by a wide margin (0.353 to 0.843 cosine, depending on the model). The reinsurance paraphrase, written in genuine industry vocabulary, ranks below the random train-departure control on three of the four models. Even ada-002, the workhorse of most enterprise RAG deployments, puts the train timetable 0.010 ahead of the specialist sentence. Only 3-large gives the specialist sense a 0.006 lift over the control, well inside the noise of the measurement.

This is the most direct failure mode there is: the embedding space does not encode the specialist sense of pool. A reranker stacked on top would not help, because the candidate scores it would re-evaluate are themselves noise. A bigger embedding model would not help, because the model that saw the swimming pool a million times and the reinsurance pool maybe a hundred times will keep weighting the swimming sense.

pool is in fact a soft OOV case: the swim sense and the risk sense share a register and 3-large catches some signal. The harder cases are strict OOV terms: ShieldPro Elite (a fictional product tier), Solvency II Article 7 (a real regulatory citation), ZRX-2025 (an internal product code). For these the embedding has no anchor at all. The model treats them as random byte strings; ranking them against any other text is a coin flip biased by tokenization quirks.

The fix is the expert who knows the vocabulary, codified as a keyword dictionary that the retriever runs against instead of the raw vector store.

The sections that follow walk through the structural failures that show up even when the term is in the model. The pool case is the simpler break that comes first.

2.2 The structural break: term similarity, not answer relevance

Section 2.1 covered the case where the term isn't in the model. From here on we cover the harder case: the term is in the model, and the embedding still returns the wrong chunk. Those failures share one structural root. An embedding sees text and ranks it by term similarity. It does not represent the question-to-answer relation at all. Two of the simplest queries you can ask make this concrete. They aren't enterprise edge cases, they're the most general questions in the world.

Yes/no question. The bare keyword Termination beats the actual Yes answer on every model

"Yes" is the right answer to a yes/no question. It never wins. The literal copy of the query's noun does. On every model from 2014 to 2024.

A subtlety worth naming: This particular failure is less harmful in practice than it looks. For a yes/no question, what we want from retrieval is not the literal word yes. We want the evidence about the topic: the page where the rule lives. The answer-phase LLM produces yes/no from that evidence. So retrieval pulling Termination or Termination may be required. (the topical matches) rather than Yes, it is possible. is closer to the right behaviour than the demo's verdict suggests. The principle the article keeps returning to is here too: the retrieval phase is not the answer phase, and they have to be separated and optimised as two distinct steps. Question parsing (Article 6), retrieval (Article 7), and generation (Article 8) develop the separation.

The failure is sharper on the next example, where retrieval needs to find the answer-bearing line.

Now the cleanest factoid in the world: "What is the capital of France?" The internet has seen "Paris is the capital of France" millions of times. If question-answer mapping showed up anywhere in any embedding space, this is where it would show up.

Query Capital of France. Paris never wins; topic-decoys sharing Capital of or France always do

Paris is never #1. On three of the four models (GloVe, ada-002, 3-large) the winner is Capital of Italy, the candidate that shares the literal phrase Capital of with the query. On MiniLM a different decoy wins: France is in Europe., because it shares the token France. Different decoys, same root cause: topic similarity, not answer relevance. Going from a 300-dim 2014 bag-of-word-vectors model to a 3072-dim 2024 OpenAI model does not flip the trap. For a factoid question, retrieval should fetch the line that contains the answer. Instead, every model picks the line that matches the query's vocabulary topically.

Does framing it as a real question help? A natural reflex is to rephrase the query as a full sentence, "What is the capital of France?", the way a user would actually type it. If the question-answer bias the modern embeddings pick up at training were strong, this is exactly where it should kick in.

Query What is the capital of France?. Paris still loses to topic decoys on every model

Read the two figures side by side. The full-sentence framing nudges Paris up a little on the OpenAI models (the question-passage training bias is real), but never enough to flip the ranking. The topic decoys keep winning. The lesson holds: how the question is phrased does not fix retrieval that is structurally pointed at the wrong target. The fix has to happen earlier in the pipeline (question parsing, Article 6) or later (re-ranking with a model that actually knows about Q-A pairs), not at the embedding step.

One more thing: Modern embedding models train on question-passage pairs (MS MARCO, Natural Questions, BEIR). This does push answer-bearing passages a little closer to the questions they answer. The bias exists. It is weak. On very general factoids it sometimes flips the decision. On specialised vocabulary the model never saw at training (internal product codes, expert terminology, contract jargon), the bias vanishes. Topic similarity dominates again.

The sections that follow catalogue this root cause in four concrete failure shapes (negation, magnitudes, topical proximity, signal dilution), plus a survey of the obvious cases (section 2.7) and a synthesis on a real PDF (section 2.8). Each is the same mechanism applied to a different query type.

2.3 Negation

A negation question turns the logical relation upside down: the user wants the candidate that is the complement of the topic, not the candidate that is closest to the topic. Embeddings can't do that. They measure topical proximity, not logical complementation. The starker the test, the clearer the failure.

Query: "What is NOT a city?" Four candidates: three are real entities (two specific cities + the literal word City), and one is Table, a mundane object that happens to be the only candidate that answers the question correctly.

Query What is NOT a city?. Every model ranks the correct answer last; negation is invisible

Every model fails the same way. The candidates that match the topic (City, Paris, New York) are on top, and Table, the one candidate that answers the question, lands last. The query word NOT carries almost no signal in the embedding space: the embedding sees a bag containing "city" and ranks anything city-related higher than anything that isn't. The fix isn't a stronger embedding model. It's a step that detects the negation at question-parsing time and inverts the retrieval (Article 6).

"Sure, but no real user writes a negation query." A reasonable objection that holds for a moment and then breaks in production. Users do not pose "what is NOT a city?" They pose "what is the premium amount on this policy?" The system returns the deductible by mistake. The user, frustrated, naturally tries to correct: "I want the premium amount, not the deductible." That second query is a negation, and it is exactly the moment a real enterprise user writes one.

The instinct is reasonable: a human reader treats not as an exclusion. The embedding does the opposite. By adding deductible to the query, even prefixed with not, the embedding pulls deductible-bearing lines closer, not further. The user's correction makes the failure strictly worse than the original query.

This is the larger principle the section keeps returning to: the raw question is never the right input to the retriever. The fix is upstream, in question parsing: negation gets detected, lifted out of the prose, encoded as a structured exclude-filter, and applied after retrieval, not embedded with the rest of the query. What the retriever should consume is a structured representation (keywords, filters, exclusions), not the user's free-form sentence.

2.4 Magnitudes and thresholds

Numerical comparisons, dates, contract amounts, account balances. Anything where the answer depends on the value itself. Take a stripped-down version: query find value greater than 1M, four candidates that are bare amounts.

Query asks for value > 1M. 1M wins everywhere; 3B, the only correct answer, ranks last

Every model picks 1M, the candidate that equals the threshold but does not strictly exceed it. The win is pure lexical match: the literal 1M token is in the query. 3B, the one candidate that answers the question, lands at #4 (dead last) on both ada-002 and 3-large. The embedding has no concept of magnitude. It sees 1M next to 1M and that wins.

This generalizes to any value-comparison or threshold question: monetary thresholds, dates ("after 2020"), durations ("longer than 30 days"), counts. Embeddings are bad at this almost by design. They compress meaning into dense vectors, and the discriminating signal (the value itself, or the operator that picks among values) is exactly what compression destroys.

The fix is well known: BM25 (the keyword-matching algorithm that weights rare words more than common ones, used in virtually every classic search engine), plus a question-parsing step that lifts the operator and the threshold out as structured fields (Article 6) so a downstream filter can do the comparison.

2.5 Topical proximity vs answer relevance

User question: "Who signed the contract?" The corpus has one passage describing how contracts must be signed (authorized representative, signature requirements) and one passage with the actual signature ("Signed: John Smith, Marketing Director, dated 2025-03-15"). The first passage talks about signing; the second is the signature. Which one wins?

Who signed the contract?. The procedural passage about signing outranks the actual signature line

This is the structural failure that the model gradient does not fix. Embedding similarity measures topical proximity, not question-to-answer relationship. A page that talks about a topic will often score higher than a page that answers a question about the topic. Definitions outscore values. Background sections outscore conclusions. Procedures outscore the concrete instances they describe.

Three of four models confirm the pattern here (GloVe, ada-002, 3-large). MiniLM is the exception: its sentence-pair training pushes the concrete-answer phrasing slightly higher than the procedural one. The pattern is stable on the other three, and reproduces across most factoid-against-procedure pairs we have tried.

2.6 Signal dilution in long context

The previous tests used candidates roughly the length of the query. Real corpus pages are not. A real page is 300-500 words, dense with details, with the answer to a specific question buried in one sentence somewhere in the middle. When you embed the whole page as a single vector, the signal of that one answer-bearing line gets averaged with everything else, and the page-level embedding drifts toward the centroid of the surrounding noise.

The cleanest way to see this is a one-variable experiment. Keep the answer sentence fixed. Prepend it with an increasing number of unrelated office-life sentences (office hours, parking rules, HR boilerplate, nothing about deductibles or water damage). Score against a fixed control candidate that shares no specific term with the query, just lives in the same broad insurance/claims vocabulary.

  • Query: deductible for water damage claims
  • Answer (varied): For water damage claims, the standard deductible is $500. prepended with N ∈ {0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16} unrelated sentences
  • Control (constant across N): Claims must include photographs, repair estimates, and police reports where applicable.

Answer signal vs noise: prepending unrelated sentences makes the answer score collapse on every model

Each model fails in its own time, but they all fail. GloVe collapses after a single extra sentence. MiniLM lasts four before the surrounding noise drags the embedding away from the answer. ada-002 and 3-large last the longest, but once the candidate reaches 144 words (eight unrelated sentences), the right answer ranks below a candidate that does not contain deductible, water, or damage at all. Embedding a 300-word page is the production version of this experiment. The takeaway: once eight or so unrelated sentences surround the answer, even the best model returns the wrong page.

This is why production pipelines that embed at the page level frequently miss the right chunk even when the answer is on it. The page-vector averages 300-500 words of topical noise around one or two answer-bearing lines. The architectural fix: embed line by line, not page by page. Only aggregate up to the page when generation needs the surrounding context. The right line on a noisy page becomes findable again because its embedding is not averaged with everything else.

2.7 The obvious cases (no demo needed)

Some query types break embeddings so plainly that a four-model comparison would just repeat the same result. They are listed here for completeness, and to make a broader point: no embedding upgrade rescues them. The fix is upstream (question parsing, Article 6) or in a different tool entirely (BM25, metadata filter, aggregation pipeline).

  • OOV identifiers and internal jargon: contract references (Section 4.2.1), regulatory citations (GDPR Art. 17.3), invoice numbers, ticket IDs, internal product names (ShieldPro Elite, SAP-MRP, KPI-Q4-V3). The embedding treats them as opaque sequences and cannot rank them semantically. Fix: BM25 or an exact-match index for the lookup, plus a glossary that maps aliases to canonical terms (ShieldPro Elite → top-tier homeowners plan) maintained as expert keywords (Article 6).
  • Boolean composition: "documents reviewed by Alice but not by Bob", "claims with damage and witness". Bag-of-words averaging erases the logical operators. Fix: parse the question into a structured filter (Article 6) and apply it after retrieval.
  • Counting and aggregation: "How many contracts did Alice sign?", "List all open claims". Embeddings return one most-similar passage; a counting answer needs a full scan or a SQL-style query over an index. Fix: route these to an aggregation pipeline (Articles 15-20).
  • Temporal predicates: "the latest version", "claims filed after 2020", "policies expiring before December". Embeddings do not represent temporal order. Fix: extract the temporal filter at question-parsing time and apply it as a metadata filter on the index.
  • Multi-hop reasoning: "Who is the manager of the person who signed contract X?" Each hop is a separate retrieval; the embedding gives you one shot. Fix: an agentic chain, or a graph traversal over a properly indexed corpus.

The pattern is consistent. When an embedding fails clearly, the answer is rarely "buy a bigger embedding model". It is "lift the query out of the embedding lane and into the right tool".

2.8 The same failures, page by page on a real PDF

The four failures above were shown on hand-written candidates. They show up identically when retrieval runs page-by-page on a real document. We embed every page of Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al. 2017; arXiv non-exclusive distribution license, declared on the arXiv abstract page; 15 pages) and run three questions; each exposes a different ranking pathology at page granularity.

What each result shows.

  • Q1, barely wins: Three pages within 0.01 of each other; the right page (page 7, where the Adam learning-rate formula lives) wins by 0.007. That is the margin of luck, not retrieval: a topical-proximity failure (section 2.5), compounded by a tight score gap.
  • Q2, top-3 saves us: Page 8 outranks page 9, but the answer (Table 3, the row that lists the alternative d_k values tested in the ablation study) lives on page 9. Top-3 is enough; top-1 would have failed silently. The discriminating signal is a numeric value inside a table: the magnitude failure (section 2.4) firing again.
  • Q3, total failure: The answer page (page 8, label-smoothing ε_ls = 0.1) falls out of the top-3 entirely. A later page full of unrelated formula symbols sneaks in instead because the embedding cannot tell one usage of the same symbol from another: the compound-polysemy problem from section 1.5, firing on the symbol ε.

Same failure categories, scaled up to a real document.

3. The one mechanism behind wins and failures

Every result in this article, wins in section 1 and failures in sections 2.1 through 2.8, comes from one mechanism. Embeddings are not doing question-answering. They are not understanding meaning. They rank text by how tokens co-occurred in the model's training corpus.

cancel and termination land close because the model saw them together a million times in similar contexts (customer support pages, legal templates, insurance FAQs). polciy lands close to policy because their sub-word pieces overlap almost completely. Capital of France ranks with Capital of Italy above Paris because the two candidates literally share the phrase Capital of and the model rewards that.

That is the whole trick. Memorized co-occurrence dressed as understanding.

The reranker uses the same mechanism. Every conversation about improving RAG that ends in "add a reranker" or "train a better reranker on your domain" runs into the same question, and almost nobody has a real answer for it: why does a reranker work? Ask three engineers, most say "machine learning, more training". That is not an explanation. A reranker is a cross-encoder that scores a query-passage pair by how strongly the tokens on both sides co-occurred in question-passage pairs during training. It is the same kind of memorized association as the bi-encoder embedder, just conditioned on the pair. If the domain vocabulary was in the training corpus at the right frequency, the reranker looks smart. If not, it inherits the failures we cataloged. The reranker does not think. It rewards keyword co-occurrence learned during training.

Where embeddings are structurally the right tool. Two cases justify the vector pass by themselves, regardless of what a keyword-based pipeline can offer:

  • Typos in the query. A single LLM call fixes the query and you are back to clean keyword search. No embedding required.
  • Typos and OCR errors in the documents. Scanned contracts, hand-typed notes, older PDFs where the OCR turned policy into pol1cy. Keyword search cannot match a corrupted token. A line-level embedding still lands in the right neighbourhood because sub-word tokenization absorbs the error. This is the case where vector search is structurally irreplaceable.

Outside those two cases, the embedding is not the retriever; it is the discovery step.

Why enterprise vocabulary breaks the mechanism. The wins in section 1 exploited co-occurrence patterns the model saw enough times to memorize: general-English synonyms, popular cross-lingual pairs, common compound terms. Enterprise vocabulary is different. Force majeure and act of God mean the same thing in an insurance contract, but the model saw them in different training contexts (contract templates vs religious texts) and does not rank them close. Rescission and cancellation are the same event in a policy, but only if the reader knows insurance vocabulary. Non-employee labor is contractor in your HR document; the model does not know that.

Enterprise synonyms are domain-specific equivalences that only your experts know. The model, trained on the public web, has generic associations that partially overlap and mostly do not. That is why picking a bigger model does not work: a bigger model has more generic associations, not the specific ones your corpus needs.

The production answer is not a better model. It is to control which associations count.

Embed at the line level so the answer's signal is not averaged with 300 words of noise. Feed the retriever text that looks like the answer, whether by HyDE or by direct keyword extraction. Use the embedding as a discovery tool to surface candidate synonyms from your corpus, let the domain expert validate, and let that curated dictionary become the actual retrieval index. The expert's knowledge, encoded once, beats the model's guesses at every subsequent query. It is auditable, faster, and durable across model releases.

The reflex for engineers coming from an ML background is to fix the model: more training data, fine-tune, upgrade providers, run a sweep. That reflex wastes months on the part of the system that was not broken. Article 3 (RAG is not machine learning) makes that case directly.
