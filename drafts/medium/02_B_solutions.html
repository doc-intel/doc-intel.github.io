How to Actually Use Embeddings in Production: Line-Level Search, HyDE, and the Expert Dictionary

The three-part production pattern that turns embedding failures into an enterprise-grade retrieval index: line-level anchors, hypothetical-answer keyword extraction, and a keyword dictionary curated with domain experts

The usual advice for weak RAG retrieval is to upgrade the embedding model or add a reranker. That advice targets the wrong layer. A bigger model moves the score by a few decimals and changes which questions fail, and a reranker only re-scores candidates the embedder already returned, so it cannot rescue an answer-bearing line that never entered the top-k. Take a single 300-word page embedded into one vector: the one sentence that answers the question gets averaged with 299 words of noise, and nothing downstream can recover a signal that retrieval never surfaced.

None of this means embeddings are useless. It means they belong at a different point in the pipeline than most stacks put them, and the fix is not a model swap. On a corpus of 100k pages, the answer on line 4 of page 87 is either found or lost at the retrieval step, before any reranker or generation prompt gets a turn.

The production answer is three moves, in this order:

  1. Embed line by line, not page by page. A 300-word page vector averages one answer-bearing sentence with 299 words of noise; the answer's signal drowns. A per-line vector isolates the signal so the retriever can find it.
  2. Feed the retriever text that looks like the answer, not the raw question. HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) does this with a fictional answer; the real value is the keywords the fictional answer contains.
  3. Discover the corpus's vocabulary with the domain expert. Embeddings surface candidate synonyms line by line; the expert validates; the validated aliases become a keyword dictionary. That dictionary (not the embedder) is what production retrieval runs against at query time.

The article runs each move on the same four models (a cheap one, two hosted flagships, one local): line-level search in section 1, HyDE in section 2, the expert dictionary in section 3. Section 4 adds a decision tree for when to skip embeddings entirely (some queries never belong in the vector lane), section 5 treats the dictionary as an enterprise asset, and section 6 covers sentiment-heavy corpora (HR feedback, support tickets) where the same discovery loop applies to emotional vocabulary.

This is the practical half of the embeddings chapter in Enterprise Document Intelligence, a series that builds enterprise RAG brick by brick from a minimal four-brick pipeline up to a full corpus. The companion piece mapped the predictable failure modes of embedding retrieval; this one turns those failures into a production-grade index.

1. Embed line by line, not page by page

The simplest way to think about embeddings: vector search is keyword search that handles synonyms, typos, and other languages, applied line by line. It is not magic. It is not "page-level semantic understanding". On a single line, the model treats cancel and terminate as close. It absorbs polciy as policy. It bridges prime annuelle and annual premium across languages. Every reliable retrieval win comes from this: a fuzzy Ctrl-F at the line level.

When you embed a whole page into a single vector, the signal of one good line gets averaged with the rest. A quick one-variable experiment makes it visible: take one answer sentence, prepend it with N unrelated office-life sentences, and re-run cosine each time. Every model (GloVe, MiniLM, ada-002, 3-large) collapses as N grows. GloVe collapses after one extra sentence. MiniLM lasts four. Even ada-002 and 3-large fail once the candidate reaches roughly 144 words. Embedding a 300-word page is that experiment, running silently on every query. So embed line by line. Only aggregate up to the page when generation needs the surrounding context.

Page-level embedding still earns its place in a few cases: when no single line carries the keyword (the page is about car insurance but never uses that phrase), when the topic is implied by surrounding vocabulary (medical page mentioning A1C / insulin / blood sugar but never diabetes), when style or register matters, when the heading is generic ("Notes", "Section 5"). Outside those cases, line-level wins almost every time.

The demo below makes it concrete on a real paper. The previous sections embedded short hand-written candidates. Here we embed every line of the Attention Is All You Need paper (15 pages, ~1000 lines) and search by a short keyword anchor. The top-K results are lines, with their page and line number. You can read each match and see why it matched: the anchor's keyword or a clear paraphrase is right there in the text.

Five operations on top of pandas and numpy: encode the query, stack the line embeddings into a matrix, batch-compute cosine in one matmul, sort by similarity, return the top-k. No vector database, no framework, no infra. The "vector store" is a DataFrame column plus a numpy dot product.

Two points from the line-level demo.

1. The matched lines show why each one matched. No magic, no ranking opacity. Every top result contains either the anchor's keyword or a clear paraphrase of it. That is line-level embedding in one phrase: a fuzzy, synonym-tolerant Ctrl-F over the document.

2. The matched line is an anchor, not the passage you send to generation. The line is a small thing the retriever can confidently locate. The passage that goes to the LLM is usually larger: the surrounding paragraph, the section, sometimes the whole page. Article 7 (retrieval) develops this as a two-step pattern: detect anchors first (line-level, keyword-level, structure-level), then choose a passage around each anchor based on what the question needs. Targeted retrieval = small N around a sharp anchor, not 30 fuzzy pages thrown at the LLM.

Cost of line-level vs page-level. A concern that comes up: embedding every line multiplies the number of vectors by roughly 10x per page. On a 100k-page corpus, that is one million line vectors instead of one hundred thousand page vectors. Storage cost is real (a few GB at 3072 dims fp16) but not prohibitive. Query latency at line level is the same order as page level with a modern vector database (FAISS, pgvector, Qdrant) because the search step is dominated by the top-k sort, not the vector count. And the win is enormous: an answer on line 4 of page 87 is now retrievable, where the page-level embedding buried it under 300 words of adjacent noise. If corpus size makes line-level truly infeasible, embed at the paragraph level (sub-page, still much smaller than a full page). Page-level as the default is a mistake made by early tutorials that has since propagated.

2. HyDE: search what the answer would contain, not the question

Embeddings don't see questions; they see term similarity. Ask "What is the capital of France?" against a candidate pool that includes Paris is the capital of France. and Capital of Italy is Rome., and every model from GloVe to 3-large ranks the second one on top because it shares the literal phrase Capital of with the query. The topic wins; the answer loses. So stop feeding the question into the retriever. Feed it text that looks like the answer instead. That's the idea behind HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings). Write (or have an LLM write) a sentence that plausibly answers the question, in the vocabulary the document would use, and embed that. The retriever compares the hypothetical-answer vector to the corpus.

The point everyone makes about HyDE is the embedding side: "the rewritten query lands in the document's neighbourhood instead of the user's". That's true and it helps. But the real value of HyDE, especially in enterprise contexts, is on a different layer. Writing a hypothetical answer is also an extraction step: it brings out the keywords the answer would contain. "Termination procedures", "rights of rescission", "cancellation fee". These are the words that anchor the search, whether the retriever is vector-based or keyword-based.

Raw query ranks target #4; HyDE rewrite injects doc vocabulary, target climbs to #1

Why HyDE worked here, and what did the work. The raw query says cancel. The target line says rescission and terminate. Zero shared content tokens. Three lexical decoys in the candidate pool each repeat cancel/cancellation several times, and together they push the formal target down to rank #4. The HyDE rewrite is a fictional answer that happens to contain rescission, terminate, written notice, renewal, the exact vocabulary the target uses. Once those tokens enter the query side, the ranking flips and the target climbs to #1.

The dominant factor is the keywords the rewrite contains. Register matching (the rewrite's formal tone aligning with the document's) and latent associations from the LLM's training contribute smaller, second-order effects. Article 6 (question parsing) decomposes them. In enterprise vocab-bounded corpora, those effects do not move the result. Run keyword search on the term set {rescission, terminate, written notice, renewal} and you get the same target with no embedding pass at all.

HyDE is implicit keyword expansion routed through an embedding step. The LLM writes a full hypothetical answer, the system embeds it, the retriever runs cosine over the corpus. All of that work to inject a handful of keywords into the query. Two simpler paths do the same vocabulary lift, explicitly:

  1. Ask the LLM for the keywords directly. One prompt: "What terms would the answer to this question contain in a typical insurance contract?" Output: rescission, terminate, written notice, renewal. Use them in keyword search. No fictional document, no embed, no cosine.
  2. Have the expert hand you the dictionary. Lawyers, claims adjusters, compliance officers already know that cancellation in user vocabulary equals rescission in contract vocabulary. Codifying that mapping once is durable; asking the LLM to rediscover it on every query is wasteful.

Both paths beat the HyDE pipeline on three fronts:

  • Auditability: the matched keywords are visible to the team and to a regulator; a 0.83 cosine score is not.
  • Latency: one LLM call, no embed round-trip per query.
  • Durability: the keywords persist in a dictionary, reusable across queries; HyDE regenerates the hypothesis from scratch every time.

Article 6 (Question Parsing) formalises this as the explicit expert keyword dictionary that grows with the corpus.

Consumer vs enterprise: On consumer-shaped corpora (general insurance FAQs, e-commerce help, public-service forms), the LLM has seen plenty of training text in the right register, so its keyword guess is usually decent. HyDE works without an expert in the loop. On enterprise corpora (internal product codes, regulatory citations, contract jargon, custom acronyms), the LLM defaults to generic legalese ("...will be outlined in the terms and conditions...") and misses the doc's actual vocabulary. The expert already knows that vocabulary. Asking the LLM to guess what the expert can hand you, on every single query, is the slow path.

3. Discover keywords with the expert: the production workflow

The standard advice ("use embeddings for semantic retrieval") is too vague. A sharper question is when do they earn their slot in the pipeline? Four answers, each pointing somewhere different.

Already know the right keywords? Use keyword search. It is faster, cheaper, auditable, and not opaque the way a vector match is. If a regulator asks why a particular passage was retrieved, "the line contains force majeure and pandemic" is a defensible answer. "The cosine similarity was 0.83" is not.

Typos in the query? Fix the query. A single LLM call corrects polciy to policy and you're back to clean keyword search. No embedding pipeline required.

Typos in the documents? Now embeddings earn their place. OCR'd contracts, scanned forms, hand-typed notes. Keyword search cannot match a misspelled token, but a line-level embedding still lands in the right neighbourhood. This is the case where vector search is structurally irreplaceable.

Multilingual corpus? Same answer, different mechanism. Contracts in French, correspondence in English, regulatory annexes in German. A multilingual embedding lets the user query in one language and retrieve lines from the others. prime annuelle finds Annual premium: $1,200. because the multilingual model places the French and English phrasings close in vector space. Maintaining bilingual keyword dictionaries by hand is possible but expensive; the multilingual embedding bridges the languages for free, and the expert keeps the dictionary working in one language with embeddings as the cross-language fallback. Requires a multilingual model: ada-002, 3-large, BGE-M3 work; GloVe and English-only sentence encoders do not.

Synonyms specific to your enterprise that you don't know yet? This is the most production-relevant case, and where embeddings are most useful: as a discovery mechanism, not as the retriever itself.

The reason matters: In legal, medical, insurance, financial corpora, the meaningful synonyms aren't dictionary synonyms. Force majeure and act of God mean the same thing in a contract, but the embedding model doesn't know that. They're not lexical neighbours and not embedding-space neighbours either. They're business-specific equivalences that only experts (lawyers, claims adjusters, compliance officers) know.

Concrete pairs across domains: What "domain synonyms" look like in practice:

  • Insurance contracts: cancellation ↔ rescission, termination, lapse of cover, surrender of the policy. deductible ↔ excess (UK), franchise (FR). claim ↔ loss notification, incident report. policyholder ↔ insured, assured, named party.
  • Medical records: blood sugar ↔ glycemia, A1C, HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose. heart attack ↔ myocardial infarction, MI, acute coronary event. high blood pressure ↔ hypertension, elevated BP reading.
  • Legal and contract clauses: force majeure ↔ act of God, unforeseeable circumstances, events beyond reasonable control. non-compete ↔ restrictive covenant, restraint of trade clause. confidentiality ↔ non-disclosure, NDA, proprietary information clause.
  • HR and employment: dismissal ↔ termination of employment, separation, severance event. salary ↔ compensation, base pay, gross remuneration. harassment ↔ unwanted conduct, hostile environment, inappropriate behaviour.

None of these aliases are dictionary synonyms in the usual sense. They are domain-specific equivalences validated by an insurance underwriter, a clinician, a contract lawyer, an HR professional. The embedding finds them as candidates; the expert says yes or no. Force majeure equals act of God only if you know it does.

HyDE makes this implicit (the LLM invents the document's likely vocabulary on the fly, section 2 showed where it falls short). The series makes it explicit: a curated keyword dictionary maintained by domain experts.

One seed query over seven candidates: the four real aliases rank top-4 on three of four models

The pattern is the discovery workflow at work. The model lists candidates ranked by similarity. The expert reads them, keeps rescission, termination, lapse of cover, surrender of the policy, drops premium payments and the other off-topic lines, and the dictionary entry for cancellation is built in one review pass. From that point on, retrieval is keyword search on the dictionary.

The workflow is progressive and runs with the experts, not around them. First few queries on a new corpus, run embeddings line-by-line as in section 1. They reveal document phrasings nobody anticipated: the contract uses non-employee labor where the user said contractor; the medical record uses A1C where the user said blood sugar level; the procedure manual uses section 4.2 where the user said overtime rule. Capture those phrasings as keyword aliases in a growing dictionary, with the expert validating each one (they know which aliases are real equivalences and which are coincidences).

Subsequent queries go through keyword search with the enriched dictionary, no embedding call needed. Each retrieval is now auditable (we know which keywords matched), faster (no LLM/embedding latency on the hot path), and the dictionary itself becomes a durable enterprise asset that survives engineering turnover.

The reframing is sharper than the standard one. Embeddings aren't the production retriever. They're the bootstrap that builds the production retriever, one keyword alias at a time, in collaboration with the people who already know the corpus. 4. When to skip embeddings entirely: a decision tree

Every query does not belong in the vector lane. The decision to reach for embeddings should be earned, not the default. Here is the tree we apply on a new question:

  1. Is the answer a lookup by exact identifier? (contract number 4287-A, GDPR Article 17.3, product SKU ZRX-2025.) Use BM25 or an exact-match index. Embeddings fail on out-of-vocabulary identifiers (the model treats them as opaque byte strings and ranks them at random) and even if it worked the exact-match index is faster and audit-friendly.
  2. Is the answer a numeric comparison or aggregation? (amounts greater than 1M, contracts signed after 2020, count of open claims.) Route to a structured query. Numbers, dates, and set operations do not belong in cosine space.
  3. Does the query invert its topic (negation, exclusion, complement)? Parse the negation out at the question-parsing step (Article 6). Apply the negation as an exclude filter after retrieval, not inside the embedding query.
  4. Is the corpus vocabulary in your expert dictionary? Use keyword search on the dictionary. Faster, auditable, no LLM in the hot path. This covers the majority of enterprise queries once the dictionary is mature.
  5. Is the query in a language the corpus is not written in, and the multilingual model is known-good? Embed. This is the case where vector search is structurally irreplaceable.
  6. Is the query worded in user vocabulary that never appears in the corpus, and the dictionary does not yet cover it? Embed at the line level, expose the candidate aliases, add the validated ones to the dictionary. Next time this query type shows up, it goes through keyword search.

The tree runs at question-parsing time (Article 6). One implication worth naming: the majority of production queries end up in keyword-search-on-dictionary, not embeddings. Embedding is the discovery step, not the retrieval step.

5. The dictionary as an enterprise asset

The keyword dictionary is not a config file. It is a domain artifact that keeps growing over the life of the corpus, and it needs the same discipline as any other data asset the business relies on.

Ownership. A named subject-matter expert per domain (insurance risk manager, senior lawyer, clinical lead) signs off on additions. The engineering team maintains the schema and the tooling; they do not curate the terms.

Versioning. Every change is a git commit with an author + a comment linking to the retrieval case that triggered it. When a retrieval later goes wrong, the audit trail shows which entry was added, when, why, and by whom.

Review cadence. A quarterly review with the expert removes stale aliases (a regulatory term deprecated by a new directive, a product name retired). The dictionary shrinks as often as it grows.

Format. Structured YAML or JSON, one file per domain, one entry per canonical concept:

  • concept: cancellation
  • aliases: [rescission, termination, lapse of cover, surrender of the policy]
  • source: insurance-contract-corpus-v1
  • curator: Marie D. (senior underwriter)
  • added: 2026-05-14
  • notes: rescission is used specifically for cancellation within the 14-day cooling-off window

That last field, notes, is where domain nuance survives. A junior engineer six months later does not need to re-learn that rescission has a specific legal meaning; the dictionary entry carries it.

Migration story. The dictionary is portable across retrieval engines. Replace Elasticsearch with OpenSearch, replace OpenSearch with Qdrant, the YAML files travel unchanged. The dictionary outlives every specific implementation of the retriever. The vector store cache, by contrast, has to be rebuilt every time the embedding model changes.

Onboarding new experts. A new claims adjuster joining the team reads the dictionary and knows what vocabulary the system currently handles. No opaque model, no "just try queries and see what works". The dictionary is the interface between domain knowledge and retrieval capability, and it reads like a glossary.

6. The HR and customer-feedback case

Most enterprise documents aren't sentiment-heavy. Contracts, regulatory texts, financial reports, technical specs are factual corpora; sections 1 through 5 are built for them. A subset of enterprise corpora is different: customer survey verbatims, employee barometer comments, support ticket free-text, brand mentions on social. The vocabulary here is emotional (drained, frustrated, delighted, let down) rather than technical (force majeure, Solvency II, cedent).

The discovery workflow still applies. An HR analyst building a burnout-signal lexicon types an explicit concept they care about, say feeling overwhelmed. The embedding pulls phrasings from the corpus in the same emotional cluster. The top match below shares zero content words with the query; all four models, GloVe through 3-large, rank it #1.

Query feeling overwhelmed matches an emotional paraphrase with zero shared tokens, winning on every model

No emotional understanding here: Emotional vocabulary clusters in the model's space the way insurance vocabulary does (the model that treats fee and charge as close does the same with overwhelmed and drained, for the same reason: they co-occur in similar contexts in training). TF-IDF + logistic regression hit roughly 88% on IMDB sentiment in 2010, before contextual embeddings, because emotional words carry signal on their own. Embeddings extend that with synonymy: overwhelmed, drained, empty, hollow, on the edge are automatically close in the space, so a query in one term retrieves sentences using any of them.

A useful split for production: If sentiment classification is the goal (score each feedback entry, aggregate trends, detect crisis spikes), a dedicated sentiment model outperforms a general embedding. The dedicated model is trained for the task; the embedding is trained for similarity. For vocabulary discovery (what phrasings express distress in our corpus?), the embedding remains the right tool. It builds the lexicon the expert validates. Two tasks, two tools. Sarcasm ("Oh great, another Monday") breaks both, and reliability there needs context the verbatim usually doesn't provide.

The pattern here is the article's larger one. First impression: this looks like emergent emotional understanding. Look closer: it is keyword-similarity with a smarter notion of "close". Apply accordingly: use the model to discover the vocabulary you didn't have; don't ask it to understand the intent behind the vocabulary.

7. What did we buy

Six sections. One sentence per takeaway.

  • Line-level, not page-level. Signal dilution disappears when the vector is one sentence.
  • Search what the answer would contain, not the raw question. HyDE's real work is keyword extraction routed through an embedding step; do the extraction directly and drop the embed step where you can.
  • Discover, don't retrieve. Embeddings surface candidate aliases from the corpus; the expert validates; the dictionary is what actually retrieves at query time.
  • Skip embeddings when the query doesn't belong there. Exact identifiers, numeric comparisons, negations, aggregations, dictionary-covered queries: keyword or structured path, not vector.
  • The dictionary is an enterprise asset. Owned by the expert, versioned, reviewed, portable across retrieval engines, outlives every model change.
  • Sentiment corpora fit the same discovery loop. The vocabulary is emotional instead of technical; the workflow is identical.

The reflex for engineers from an ML background is to fix the model: more training data, fine-tune, change providers, run a sweep. That reflex wastes months on the part of the system that was not broken. Article 3 (RAG is not ML) makes that case directly: RAG is not machine learning, and the ML toolkit solves the wrong problem.

8. Sources and further reading

Reimers and Gurevych (Sentence-BERT, 2019) is the reference for what embedding a line means technically. Gao et al. (HyDE, 2023) is the technique this article reframes: the load-bearing piece is the keywords the hypothetical answer contains, not the embedding step itself; asking the LLM for the keywords directly recovers the same passage with less infrastructure. Formal et al. (SPLADE, 2021) is the closest thing in the literature to vector search is keyword search: a learned sparse retrieval that produces interpretable per-token weights instead of a dense vector, bridging the two worlds the article contrasts.

Same direction as the article:

  • Reimers & Gurevych, Sentence-BERT, EMNLP 2019 (arXiv:1908.10084). Line-level embedding as the natural granularity.
  • Gao et al., HyDE: Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, ACL 2023 (arXiv:2212.10496). The HyDE technique the article reframes: keywords from the hypothetical answer are what does the work.
  • Formal et al., SPLADE, 2021 (arXiv:2107.05720). Learned sparse retrieval; a bridge between keyword and embedding worlds.
  • Ravichander et al., CONDAQA, EMNLP 2022 (arXiv:2211.00295). Motivates the negation-detection step the decision tree runs at question-parsing time.

Different angle, different context:

  • Khattab & Zaharia, ColBERT, SIGIR 2020 (arXiv:2004.12832). Late-interaction retrieval as another way to answer the exact-token matching problem, complementary to the expert-dictionary path.
  • Wang et al., E5, 2022 (arXiv:2212.03533) and Lee et al., NV-Embed, 2024 (arXiv:2405.17428). The scale-fixes-it line: larger contrastive pre-training closes some of the OOV gap. Still doesn't fix the structural failures.

Two exercises that build the intuition fastest:

  • Take a technical term from your own domain, embed all lines of one document, look at the top-20 candidates. How many are true aliases vs. topical decoys? That ratio is the expert workload per new seed term.
  • Run the same seed against a page-level embedding of the same document. Compare the top-3 pages to the top-3 lines. The gap you see is exactly the dilution effect: page-level averages the answer's signal with 300 words of noise; line-level isolates it. The primitive from section 1 is now working in your favour instead of against you.
