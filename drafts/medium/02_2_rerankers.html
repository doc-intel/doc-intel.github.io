The standard fix for a RAG system that keeps missing answers is to add a reranker. Retrieve the top 100 passages with embeddings, then let a cross-encoder re-sort them so the best passage lands at rank 1. Almost every retrieval tutorial and vendor blog repeats it. The advice is not wrong, but it is narrower than it sounds. A reranker only reorders the passages retrieval already pulled. If weak retrieval never brought the right passage into the top 100, no cross-encoder will conjure it, because a cross-encoder scores the pairs it is handed and adds nothing new to the pool. So a reranker fixes a ranking problem, not a recall problem, and it is not free: 200 to 400 ms per query, plus a second model to deploy and keep in sync.

This article puts that advice on the bench. I ran seven models on the same retrieval cases: four embedding models spanning 2014 to 2024, plus three off-the-shelf cross-encoder rerankers. On most query shapes the reranker did not win. A 2024 embedding, and even a 22-million-parameter free model from 2021, ranked the right passage higher than the big rerankers did, and on two shapes stacking the largest reranker made the result worse. The cross-encoder earned its 300 ms on exactly one shape: an answer buried inside a long, noisy paragraph. On the rest, a hand-written dictionary of the domain's own vocabulary covered the same ground, cheaper and fully auditable.

Here is the path. Section 1 says plainly what a reranker is and where its training data stops. Section 2 lists the four kinds of keywords a good retrieval already carries, the same bridge the reranker learns statistically from labelled data. Sections 3 to 5 run the seven-model comparison case by case. Section 6 collects the shapes no cross-encoder fixes: negation, exact identifiers, listing, out-of-domain terms. Section 7 says where the reranker still earns its place.

1. What a reranker actually is

A reranker is a second reader. The plainest one-liner in the literature is the one Article M5 (delegation) puts on paper: an embedding asks "are these two texts alike?", a reranker asks "does this passage answer this question?". Same query, same candidate, two different questions. That gap is the reranker's whole reason to exist.

The retrieval brick that runs before it is the librarian. They know every book on the shelves. Ask for anything, they hand you the 20 candidate passages whose topic matches your question. They know the shelf. They have not opened any of the 20 with your specific question in mind, because they cannot: they have to pre-index every passage in the library, once, offline, so at question time they are just checking their index.

The reranker is the researcher you hand those 20 passages to. They open each one, and read it while holding your exact question in their head. They then hand back the 3 passages where the answer sits most clearly, in the phrasing closest to your question. They do not add new books. They cannot. If the librarian pulled the wrong 20 to start with, the researcher fixes nothing.

That plain version holds all the way down to what the model does. The librarian pre-compresses every passage into a summary vector once, at index time, and matches vector to vector at query time. The researcher runs a heavier model on each of the 20 candidate (question, passage) pairs at query time, one call per pair, and gives each pair a score. Two loops, two costs. One is pre-computation, the other is real-time work.

Which is where the trade-off has a shape. The librarian is fast and coarse. The researcher is slower and sharper. And there is exactly one situation where the researcher is worth calling in: when the pile the librarian pulled is big enough that its top 3 among the 20 actually matters, AND you can pay for a heavier model to read every one of them. Any other configuration and the researcher was hired for nothing.

And there is a deeper limit, structural, that decides whether the researcher can even be useful. A reranker learns its statistical mapping between question-language and answer-language from labelled (question, answer) pairs, most of them public retrieval datasets: MS-MARCO (Bing search logs, Bajaj et al. 2016), BEIR (web benchmarks, Thakur et al. 2021), a few academic corpora. Those datasets do not contain your enterprise's vocabulary. They do not contain your internal product codes, your specialised legal or insurance terms, your industry-specific phrasings, the way your users write a question in your intranet. The mapping the reranker learned covers a broad web-shaped universe, not the narrow domain you actually operate in. For the questions your users actually ask, the answer-side vocabulary the reranker was trained to recognise was never in the training data. Fine-tuning on your own labels closes the gap, but fine-tuning is a real project (data collection, labelling, retraining, evaluation), not a free upgrade. This is exactly where the series lands its central thesis: for a bounded enterprise domain, writing the expert dictionary is cheaper, faster, and covers the vocabulary the reranker was never taught. The expert holds the mapping already, from years of practice. What lives in their head as tacit expertise, the retrieval brick can carry as an explicit typed dictionary. That is what Section 2 lists next.

The rest of this article measures the trade-off empirically.

2. The four kinds of keywords a good retrieval already carries

The reranker's job, once you strip the ML vocabulary, is "bridge question-language and answer-language". That bridge already exists in any team that has an expert on the domain. What the expert carries in their head, a retrieval brick can carry in a typed dictionary of keywords, tagged into four kinds. Each kind answers a different question the retrieval has to solve, and together they close the gap the reranker was trained to close.

Kind 1. Question keywords. The words the user is likely to type. The obvious ones. If a user asks "what is the maximum coverage amount?" the question keywords are maximum coverage amount, plus the synonyms limit, cap, ceiling, plafond in French. A retrieval brick expands the raw query to hit passages that use any of the synonyms. This is exactly the layer where embeddings already do well: the semantic link is keyword-to-keyword.

Kind 2. Answer keywords. The words that appear inside the correct answer passage, and are NOT in the question. The passage that answers "what is the maximum coverage amount?" is likely to contain up to, not exceed, capped at, maximum, or a specific number followed by €, $, %. The reranker learns these statistically from labelled data. An expert types them from experience. Storing them at index time (as a tagged list per section, per question intent) is how the question-to-answer bridge stops being a mystery.

Kind 3. Format keywords. The shape the answer should take. If the user asks about a coverage amount, the answer is a currency value. If they ask about a deadline, the answer is a date or a duration. If they ask about a percentage, the answer is %. A retrieval brick that filters candidate passages by the presence of the expected format (regex [0-9]+\s€, ISO date pattern, [0-9]+\s%) skips half the false positives the reranker would have to sort out. See Article 6B (question parsing extraction) for the schema field that carries the format.

Kind 4. Entity variants. When the answer is a named entity (a country, a person, a currency, an insurer, a party to a contract), the retrieval brick benefits from a variants list for that entity type. France matches french, French Republic, FR, Française, hexagone, métropolitaine. Countries, currencies, ISO codes: all closed lists you can compile once and reuse. The reranker learns some of these variants from training data; a variants list is exhaustive and auditable.

These four kinds live together as a typed dictionary that the retrieval brick reads at query time. Question parsing (Article 6) extracts which kind is needed for a given question; retrieval (Article 7) matches the passage on all four. The reranker was trained to learn the same mapping statistically from labels. If the expert already wrote the mapping, the reranker is a fallback for the cases the expert did not cover. That framing is what the empirical tests below are measuring.

A concrete case, from Article M5. Take "what is the deductible?". The embedding returns five lines, all matching on the word or a synonym:

  1. a contents entry, "3.2 Deductibles and self-insured retentions"
  2. a cross-reference, "the deductible is set out in the schedule"
  3. a definition, "Deductible means the amount the insured bears per claim"
  4. the clause itself, "the insured bears the first €1,500 of each and every loss"
  5. a brochure line, "low deductibles available on request"

All five contain the keyword. Only line 4 answers the question, because line 4 carries the shape of an answer: an amount, a currency, a per-loss qualifier. A reranker reads the question together with each candidate and pulls line 4 to the top. The expert dictionary carries the exact same markers: Kind 3 (format: currency symbol, amount) plus Kind 2 (answer keywords: "each and every claim", "bears the first"). Where the dictionary already tagged those markers at index time, the second read has already happened. Where it did not, the reranker earns its 300ms.

2.1 Sibling technique: HyDE, or the answer-side query expansion

HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings, Gao et al. 2022, "Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels") is the technique that looks clever from a distance and, up close, does exactly what the four-kind dictionary already does. HyDE asks the LLM to write a short hypothetical answer to the question first, then embeds that made-up answer and searches with it. Why would a made-up answer help? Because a question and its answer are written in different words. "What is the deductible?" shares almost no vocabulary with "the insured bears the first €1,500 of each and every loss". Embed the bare question and you search with the question's words. Embed a hypothetical answer and you search with the answer's words, which is where the real answer actually sits.

Strip HyDE down and the effect is Kind 2 keywords, generated on the fly. The hypothetical answer helps for one reason: it contains the words the real answer contains. HyDE is a keyword generator wearing a sentence. A domain expert does the same reframing in their head before every Ctrl-F. And in a bounded enterprise domain you do not need the LLM round-trip to get it. The answer-side vocabulary is exactly what the four-kind dictionary already holds. Expand the query with those and you have HyDE's benefit, deterministically, at zero cost per call, with an auditable answer. HyDE was another way to bridge from question keywords to answer keywords; the dictionary was there first.

3. Same setup as the embeddings article, two situations

Two situations.

Scene 1: A team building a RAG system over a few hundred contracts has read Article 2 (embeddings' failure modes). Embeddings break on negation, on exact identifiers, on the gap between a question and its answer. The team's first reflex is the one the literature suggests: add a reranker. Cross-encoder, smaller than an LLM, smarter than cosine, slot it between embeddings and the LLM. They wire in bge-reranker-base (BAAI's open-source cross-encoder reranker, MIT license declared on the HuggingFace model card), send it the top-100 from the embedding stage, keep the top-10. A few queries that were broken yesterday seem to work today. The team is encouraged.

Scene 2: Two weeks in, the same operational pattern from Article 2 returns. The user asks "list every clause that mentions termination" and the system returns the three "most relevant" ones, exactly three, ranked. The contract has eleven. The user asks "what's the cancellation rule for non-employees?" The reranker has never seen the company's term non-employee labor, and ranks an unrelated paragraph on top. The user asks "is there a clause that does NOT mention indemnification?" Same negation failure as before; the cross-encoder doesn't see logical complementation any more than the embedding did. Latency, meanwhile, is now in the hundreds of milliseconds. The cross-encoder runs at query time on every candidate, and there's no way to precompute it. Worse: when they run side-by-side comparisons against text-embedding-3-large without the reranker, the embedding alone often matches or beats ada-002 + bge-reranker-base.

The classical retrieval funnel looks the same way it did in Article 2:

  • Cheap embedding similarity at the bottom narrows millions of candidates to thousands.
  • An optional cross-encoder reranker between narrows the thousands to dozens.
  • The chat-completion LLM on top reads the dozens.

The reranker is the layer that sits between two large constants on the cost-and-quality ladder. Knowing what each stage does makes the funnel work; expecting magic from any single stage is how teams lose six months.

This article tests the cost-perf gradient empirically: four embedding models from 2014 to 2024, plus three off-the-shelf cross-encoder rerankers, scored side by side on the cases Article 2 catalogued. The result is more surprising than the funnel suggests.

The seven models tested, with their license attestation URLs (the URL of the page on which the model author themselves declares the license):

  • text-embedding-ada-002 (OpenAI 2022, 1536-dim): proprietary; OpenAI Terms of Use.

4. How a reranker works: funnel, cost gradient, cross-encoder mechanics

Before the empirical tests, the architectural picture. Two reasons it matters: the reranker is a real engineering object with real costs, and the editorial position the series defends only makes sense once the classical role is on the table.

4.1 The cost/precision gradient

Three stages, ordered by cost per query:

  • Bi-encoder embedding similarity: A precomputed vector per document. At query time the model encodes the query once and runs cosine similarity against the index. Milliseconds for millions of candidates. Cheap and approximate.
  • Cross-encoder reranker: Query and passage are tokenised together and passed through a transformer that attends across both. The output is a single relevance score per pair. Cannot be precomputed because the query is part of the input. Tens of milliseconds per pair. Mid-cost, mid-precision.
  • Chat-completion LLM: Reads a small candidate set and produces a structured answer. Hundreds of milliseconds, dollars per million tokens. Most expensive, most accurate.

Each stage is justified by what it can do cheaper than the next stage above. Embeddings can't do everything an LLM can, but they can score a million candidates in the time the LLM reads ten. Rerankers can't do everything an LLM can, but they can rank a thousand candidates in the time the LLM reads twenty. That is the textbook story. Section 5 tests it on real query shapes. The gradient turns out to be flatter, and sometimes inverted, compared to what the funnel suggests.

4.2 The funnel

The architectural picture is a funnel. The corpus has, say, 200,000 pages. The embedding stage scores them all and returns the top 100. The reranker scores the 100 and returns the top 10. The LLM reads the 10 and produces an answer. Each arrow narrows the candidate pool by an order of magnitude or more, and each stage is justified by the cost-versus-quality trade with its neighbours.

Cost grows top to bottom; candidate count shrinks; each stage hands a smaller set on

This funnel logic makes the reranker interesting only when the upstream stage produces a large pool. If you already retrieve top-5 from a well-scoped pipeline, there is no funnel to narrow: the LLM will read all five regardless of order. A reranker on 100 candidates that the LLM can only read 10 of is a real gain; a reranker on 5 candidates the LLM will read anyway adds nothing.

On paper, the funnel is elegant: three mathematically distinct scorers, each tuned to its rung of the cost-versus-quality ladder, each justified by the trade with its neighbours.

In practice, the elegance does not transfer to the people the system is built for. A business expert who opens an audit log sees three different scores per page, each on a different scale, each produced by a model they do not understand and cannot reproduce. The system becomes harder to explain than the documents it is supposed to answer questions on.

The editorial position the series defends (developed in Section 7) is not that the funnel is wrong on paper. It is that the architectural moves the experts can audit (expert vocabulary, structure-aware retrieval, classify-before-retrieve, specific pipelines per question type) buy more trust per dollar than stacking statistically distinct scorers does.

4.3 Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder mechanically

The mechanical difference matters for what each can model. A bi-encoder (the embedding model from Article 2) encodes the query and the passage independently, then compares vectors. The two never see each other inside the model. Whatever interaction matters between them (does this passage answer this question) has to survive the projection into a fixed-dimensional vector for each side.

A cross-encoder tokenises query and passage together, separated by a special token, and runs them through a transformer. Every word in the passage sees every word in the query at once: the model reads them side by side rather than comparing two separate summaries. In principle this lets the cross-encoder catch fine-grained signals a bi-encoder loses in projection.

In principle. The training data and objective decide what it learns to score.

5. The cost-perf gradient, tested on the same cases

The textbook funnel sells a clean cost-perf gradient: weak embeddings at the bottom, strong embeddings in the middle, cross-encoder rerankers on top. Each step costs more, each step is supposed to score more accurately. The honest test is to take the cases Article 2 catalogued and run them across the whole gradient: four embedding models from GloVe-avg (2014) to text-embedding-3-large (2024), plus three off-the-shelf cross-encoder rerankers (bge-base, bge-large, ms-marco-MiniLM-L-12-v2). Seven columns per figure. Read each row horizontally and the gradient either holds, breaks, or sometimes inverts.

Three things to watch as you scan each figure:

  • Does the TARGET row's #1 win migrate from left to right (the gradient holds, bigger model = better)?
  • Does the TARGET get stuck at #2-#3 across all seven columns (no learned scorer catches the shape)?
  • Or does a smaller, cheaper model rank the TARGET higher than the big rerankers (the gradient inverts)?

All three patterns appear below.

5.1 Literal-token trap (Article 2, section 1.6)

Query hot dog, candidates: a food paraphrase (TARGET, zero shared tokens), the lexical trap the dog basked in the hot sun, and an unrelated decoy. In Article 2, ada-002 fell for the trap; only text-embedding-3-large recovered.

The result on the seven-column grid is striking: 3-large is still the only model that flips the trap to #2 and lifts the paraphrase to #1. None of the three rerankers do. Stacking bge-large on top of ada-002 does not buy you what 3-large already gives you for free at the embedding stage. If the budget is "either upgrade the embedding or add a reranker," this case argues for upgrading the embedding.

For query hot dog, each column's #1 row shows whether the scorer picked the paraphrase or the trap

5.2 Synonym recovery with a hard lexical distractor (Article 2, section 1.2)

Query is green card needed. The right answer (Permanent resident card is required for this process.) shares zero tokens with the query but is the strict synonym. The trap (Green colored cards are popular in stationery stores.) shares THREE tokens (green, card, cards) and is semantically unrelated. This is the canonical "synonym vs lexical overlap" test.

The grid shows an inversion of the cost-perf claim. MiniLM, ada-002, 3-large and bge-base all rank the synonym TARGET #1. Then bge-large and ms-marco-MiniLM-L-12-v2 fall back to the lexical trap at #1, as if the bigger / MS-MARCO-trained models have a stronger lexical bias. Two of the three rerankers actively make this worse than bge-base does. A team that auto-stacks the biggest available reranker on every query loses ground here that they would have kept by sticking with the small one, or by skipping the reranker entirely.

Synonym TARGET shares zero tokens, the trap shares three, and each scorer rewards meaning or token overlap

5.3 Topical proximity vs answer relevance (Article 2, section 2.5)

User question: "Who signed the contract?" The corpus has one passage describing how contracts must be signed (procedural, dense in signed/signature/representative), and one passage that is the actual signature (Signed: John Smith, Marketing Director, dated 2025-03-15). On every embedding model in Article 2, the procedural passage outranked the actual signature. This is the kind of question-answer mismatch cross-encoders are trained on (MS-MARCO is roughly this shape repeated millions of times).

The grid says something the textbook doesn't predict. MiniLM is the only model, embedding or reranker, that promotes the actual signature line to #1. Every other column, including the three cross-encoder rerankers explicitly trained on this kind of pair, leaves the procedural passage at #1 and the signature at #2. A 22M-parameter free embedding beats six other layers on the canonical reranker test. The cost-perf gradient does not just flatten here; it inverts.

The procedural passage shares more tokens but the signature line is the answer, testing topical proximity vs answer-ness

5.4 Signal dilution in long context (Article 2, section 2.6)

The same answer sentence, presented twice: once alone, once buried inside a 70-word policy paragraph. A topical decoy (talking densely about deductibles, never giving the answer) and an unrelated paragraph round out the candidates. In Article 2 every embedding model picked the short answer alone, but lost the buried-answer paragraph to the topical decoy: the surrounding noise diluted the signal of the answer sentence.

This is the one shape where the rerankers earn their cost. bge-large, bge-base and ms-marco-MiniLM all rank the short answer #1 with the buried-answer paragraph #2. They recover the buried answer to second place, where ada-002 and MiniLM had it third or worse. 3-large already gets there at the embedding stage. So the picture is: on signal dilution, either pay for 3-large at the embedding stage, or stack a free reranker on top of a cheaper embedding. Both paths work. This is the cleanest case in the article for the cross-encoder layer.

Same answer alone vs buried in a 70-word paragraph against a topical decoy

5.5 The yes/no question (Article 2, section 2.3)

Article 2's deepest case: the actual answer (Yes, it is needed.) to a yes/no question, against a literal copy of the query keywords (Permanent resident card) and a longer mix. On every embedding model, the literal-keyword copy beat the answer. The whole reason cross-encoders exist as a layer is that they are trained on query-answer pairs where the answer rarely repeats the query.

The grid mostly confirms: the literal copy Permanent resident card is #1 on every column. The TARGET (Yes, it is needed.) is #3 or #4 across all the embeddings and the BGE rerankers. The one column that promotes the actual answer is ms-marco-MiniLM-L-12-v2. It puts Yes, it is needed. at #2, ahead of A green card may be required. and the No answer. A small win, on a yes/no shape that nothing else handles. Worth knowing the MS-MARCO-trained reranker has this specific behavior; not enough to design a pipeline around.

TARGET is the yes/no answer, the trap is a literal copy of the query, testing answer above echo

Read the columns horizontally and the cost-perf gradient is mostly flat. On 2.1 the only winner is 3-large (a 2024 embedding, no reranker required). On 2.3 the only winner is MiniLM (a 22M-param free embedding from 2021). On 2.2 two of the three rerankers are worse than the smaller models. Only 2.4 (signal dilution) shows a clean reranker win. Stacking a free off-the-shelf reranker on top of a cheaper embedding does not buy reliable lift over replacing the embedding with a stronger one; on some shapes it actively hurts.

This matches a pattern engineering teams discover the hard way: the marginal dollar is better spent on the embedding stage (or, as the rest of the series argues, on upstream architecture: expert keywords, classify-before-retrieve) than on a reranker. The classical funnel sells "embeddings cheap, rerankers more accurate" as a clean ladder. On these query shapes there is no ladder. Section 6 is the harder side: cases that don't move regardless of which scorer you use.

6. Where the cross-encoder still breaks (and where the expert dictionary wins)

Four failure modes that survive the cross-encoder layer regardless of size or family. The architectural job, which the rest of the series is about, is to recognise these cases at the question-parsing stage and route them through pipelines that don't rely on similarity scoring at all.

6.1 Negation, still invisible

Article 2 ran the negation test on four embedding models: query "What is NOT a city?", candidates Paris, New York, City, Table. Every model ranked Table (the only correct answer) at the bottom. The negation token carried no signal. Does any cross-encoder pick up the inversion?

For negation the correct answer is Table, and each scorer either picks it or a city

Cross-encoders are trained on (query, relevant_passage) pairs from web search and MS-MARCO. Almost no training pair has the shape "the relevant passage is the complement of the query's topic". The model learned to score topical alignment, and a NOT in the query barely shifts that. The fix is at question-parsing time: detect the negation, invert the retrieval (Article 6).

6.2 Exact identifiers and internal acronyms

Contract reference numbers, internal product codes, acronyms that exist only inside the company. The intuition is that learned similarity will confuse ZRX-2025-A with the close-by ZRX-2024-B. Let's see.

Two contracts differ by one identifier character, and every scorer except GloVe ranks the right one

The figure is a useful lesson in test design as much as in retrieval. With only three candidates and the right contract appearing verbatim in the candidate text, every modern scorer disambiguates correctly. MiniLM, both OpenAI embeddings, and all three rerankers put ZRX-2025-A at #1. Only GloVe gets confused.

The real failure mode for identifiers is at scale: a corpus with hundreds of contracts whose surrounding text follows a templated pattern (Contract covers up to ), where the identifier is the only discriminating feature. There the embedding's literal-token signal becomes a tiny fraction of the cosine, and the close-by IDs blur.

Production-scale identifier disambiguation belongs in BM25 or an exact-match index (Article 6, section 3.1 via concept_keywords_df), not in similarity. The 3-candidate test here just shows that embeddings are not blind to identifiers when the field is small.

6.3 Listing, the canonical failure mode

The reranker's job is to rank candidates. A listing question wants all of them. Every scorer will dutifully order the eleven termination clauses from most to least relevant; the top-k cut discards the ones it ranked lowest, and the user, who asked for the complete set, gets a partial answer.

Across eleven termination clauses and every scorer, the score gradient is real but top-k silently discards real matches

The fix is listing aggregation (Article 12), not a reranker. A listing question is parsed as a list_all intent at the question-parsing stage and routed to a pipeline that returns every matching item, not the top-k by score.

6.4 Out-of-domain vocabulary

Every model on the grid was trained on public retrieval datasets. The OpenAI embeddings and the BGE rerankers learned from broad web/retrieval data; ms-marco-MiniLM-L-12-v2 learned from MS-MARCO. Specialised vocabularies (medical, legal, financial, regulatory) barely appear there, so each model's sense of "relevant" was shaped by a different kind of text from the ones your corpus contains. The BEIR benchmark (Thakur et al. 2021) documents this exact pattern across 18 out-of-domain datasets: neural retrievers that top the leaderboard on MS-MARCO drop by 5 to 15 points of nDCG@10 when transferred to specialised domains they were not trained on. Fine-tuning the reranker on domain data fixes much of this. But fine-tuning is a project, not a free upgrade. Off-the-shelf, no scorer on the grid bridges to the company term.

For query contractor overtime vs company term non-employee labor, every scorer ranks TARGET at #3

Universal failure across the seven columns. The TARGET sits at #3 on every model; Contractors are paid on a per-project basis (the surface lexical match) wins at #1. Neither the largest embedding nor the largest reranker bridges contractor → non-employee labor. This is exactly the problem the series's concept_keywords_df is built to solve. The expert curates the mapping contractor → non-employee labor, overtime → beyond 40h/week, and the retrieval stage uses those keywords directly. The reranker would need fine-tuning on the company's contracts to learn the same mapping the expert just typed.

This is also the failure mode that generates the worst class of hallucination downstream. Ji et al.'s survey of hallucination in NLG (2022) names this exact pattern as intrinsic hallucination: an output that contradicts the source it was generated from. In a RAG pipeline, when the reranker ranks a wrong-but-plausible paragraph at #1, the LLM synthesises an answer faithful to the retrieved passage but wrong for the user's real question. The audit trail shows a defensible 0.83 cross-encoder score under the answer. The user sees a confident, wrong response, sourced to a real page of the contract. When the dictionary matches instead, the audit trail shows the exact keyword that fired ("non-employee labor" → matched paragraph 4.2), not a probability, and the wrong-but-plausible passage never reaches the LLM.

7. Where rerankers justify their cost

The position of the series, stated plainly:

Cross-encoder rerankers are a fallback for narrow cases, not the primary stage of an enterprise pipeline. They are worth their cost when the candidate pool is large (top-100,000 from a vector store), the upstream is generic cosine, and there is no time to build a curated pipeline. They add little when the upstream is already small, already-scoped, and already structured.

In production enterprise RAG, three architectural moves make the reranker's value smaller than the literature suggests.

Question parsing routes the query to a specific pipeline. A listing question runs through list_all aggregation (Article 12), not through ranked retrieval. A filtering question runs through metadata filters (Article 17, querying the corpus), not through similarity scoring. A negation question is detected and inverted at question-parsing time (Article 6). The reranker's input is therefore a small, already-scoped candidate set produced by a structurally appropriate pipeline, not a top-100 dump from a generic vector store.

Classify-before-retrieve shrinks the candidate pool. Article 15 (preparing the corpus) develops the classification step that tags each document with topic, type, and date metadata. At query time, metadata filters reduce the candidate corpus from 200,000 documents to maybe 800. The reranker (if it runs at all) runs on a pool small enough that a domain expert could review it in fifteen minutes. There is no top-100,000 funnel left to manage.

Expert keywords replace probabilistic ranking on the cases that matter. Article 6 (question parsing) builds the concept_keywords_df table that maps user vocabulary to document vocabulary. The mapping is curated; it is auditable; it is exactly the work that a reranker is supposed to do probabilistically. Where the keyword dictionary covers the case, ranking is replaced by structured retrieval and the reranker's value drops further.

The legitimate large-corpus case (thousands to hundreds of thousands of documents in a vector store, single ad-hoc question, no time to build a curated pipeline) is real, and the series acknowledges it in Articles 15-20 (corpus scale). Even there, the preferred move is classify-and-filter first; the reranker comes in to disambiguate the residual pool.

The bottom line for the reader: rerankers are useful. They have a real place in the literature. The cost/precision gradient is real, and the funnel is the engineering reality of any production retrieval architecture. The series explains them and uses them where they earn their cost. But the architectural choices the series defends (expert vocabulary, structure-aware retrieval, classify-before-retrieve, specific pipelines for specific question-types) push the reranker into a narrow corner rather than the default. Article 9 (the upgraded pipeline) returns to method combination at the retrieval layer; Articles 15-20 develop the corpus-scale case.

8. Conclusion

Rerankers are a fallback for one specific failure mode (signal dilution in long context), not a primary stage. On the other shapes the funnel claims to fix (negation, exact identifiers, out-of-vocabulary terms, listing), no cross-encoder moves the needle. The marginal dollar buys more lift at the embedding stage than the reranker stage on these query shapes.

The structural moves that make rerankers mostly redundant (question parsing, classify-before-retrieve, expert keywords, intent-specific pipelines) are what the rest of the series builds. Apply Article M5's continuity test to the reranker and it reads plainly: the reranker automates the expert's second read. Once the four-kind dictionary encodes the answer-shape markers at index time, that second read has already happened, deterministically, once and for all documents. The reranker keeps its place as fallback for the answer shapes the expert did not anticipate. The dictionary handles the rest, and it does so without the class of hallucination that comes from a wrong-but-plausible passage sitting at #1 with a confident cross-encoder score under it.

9. Sources and further reading

The cross-encoder reranker family this article tests descends directly from Nogueira & Cho (Passage Re-ranking with BERT, 2019), the seminal paper that established the bi-encoder → cross-encoder funnel the BGE family inherits (Xiao et al. 2023). The structural limits surfaced empirically in Section 6 (listing failure, exact identifiers, negation) are the same ones the late-interaction literature (Khattab & Zaharia 2020) was meant to address from a different angle. LLM-as-reranker (Pradeep et al. 2023) is the next step up the cost-perf gradient; it sits beyond this article's scope but is the option that flips when frontier model costs drop further.
