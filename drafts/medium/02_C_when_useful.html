The Real Roles of Embeddings in a Production RAG Pipeline

Why the keyword dictionary is the spine, and how embeddings work as connective tissue across design, ingestion, and query time, with concrete cases where each role is irreplaceable

Ask ten teams how RAG retrieval should work and you get two reflexes. The first is "embed everything and rank by cosine." The second, from anyone who has been burned in production, is "embeddings are useless, go pure keyword." Both are too broad. In an enterprise RAG pipeline that actually holds up, the keyword dictionary curated with domain experts is the retrieval spine, and embeddings play several supporting roles the dictionary cannot fill on its own.

On a 500-page insurance corpus, the dictionary answers most queries with no vector call at all. Embeddings step in for the cases it cannot cover: a user typo like polciy renwal, an OCR artifact that turned déductible into déducctible, a product name added last week that the expert has not curated yet. Those roles are not one homogeneous thing. They span three deployment phases with very different constraints:

  • Design time: offline, one-shot, expert-validated. Build the dictionary itself.
  • Ingestion time: per document, semi-automated. Keep the corpus clean as new documents arrive.
  • Query time: per user query, unpredictable. Fill the gap when the dictionary cannot anticipate what the user typed or what the document says.

This article walks through each phase, names the specific embedding role there, and shows a concrete case where it earns its keep. The conclusion pulls the map together: keyword dictionary as spine, embedding as connective tissue.

1. The default is a keyword dictionary, curated with experts

Every article in this series builds toward one production pattern. Retrieval runs on a structured keyword dictionary that maps user vocabulary to the actual terms your documents use. The dictionary is not a config file. It is a domain artifact:

  • Owned by a named domain expert (senior underwriter, contract lawyer, clinician, HR lead).
  • Versioned in git with an author + a comment per change.
  • Reviewed on a fixed cadence (quarterly) to remove stale aliases and add new ones.
  • Portable across retrieval engines (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Qdrant all work with the same YAML files).

At query time, a user question flows through question parsing (Article 6): the question is normalised, keywords are extracted, aliases from the dictionary are added. Retrieval is then a filter on structured tables: the pages that contain the enriched keyword set win. Ranking is by count of matched keywords, boosted by concept weight, sorted by page relevance. Auditable end to end.

This pattern is fast (no LLM in the hot path), cheap (no per-query embedding round-trip), and durable (the dictionary outlives every embedding model release). It is what production teams converge on after their vector-store-only pipeline fails on real enterprise vocabulary.

Embeddings are not the retriever. But they are not useless either. They earn slots at three specific phases of the pipeline lifecycle, each with its own logic.

2. Three deployment phases, three sets of constraints

Before we walk through the roles, name the phases so it stays clear where each case belongs:

  • Design time. Before the product is in production. Offline, batch, one-shot per corpus (or per major corpus refresh). Domain expert is in the loop. Latency does not matter. Result is a persistent asset (the dictionary).
  • Ingestion time. Every time a new document enters the corpus. Semi-automated per document. Runs once per document, then the result persists. Small human loop for tagging validation. Latency matters within a few seconds per document.
  • Query time. Every time a user asks a question. Fully automated. Runs once per query. Latency budget is hundreds of milliseconds. No human in the loop. What was not anticipated has to be handled inline.

Different phases → different constraints → different acceptable uses of embeddings. The mistake is to talk about "using embeddings" as one thing. The pipeline uses them in radically different ways depending on when the vector pass runs.

3. Design time: build the dictionary itself

The dictionary does not appear out of nowhere. Someone has to construct it, and on a new corpus the expert does not know all the aliases the documents use. This is where embeddings shine as a discovery tool.

The workflow is a small loop, run once per seed term the expert cares about:

  1. The expert types a concept they care about, e.g. cancellation.
  2. The system embeds every line of the corpus (or a representative sample if the corpus is huge) and returns the top-K nearest lines by cosine.
  3. The expert reads the returned lines and keeps the aliases that are true equivalences in the domain. They drop the noise (topically related but not equivalent).
  4. The validated aliases go into the dictionary under the concept cancellation.

The embedding is a candidate generator. The expert is the judge. Neither can do the job alone: the expert did not have rescission or surrender of the policy at the top of their head; the embedding did not know that premium payments, also close in vector space, is the wrong alias.

Concrete case, insurance broker corpus. Seed the loop with cancellation on a 500-page broker corpus. The top-20 lines by cosine include: rescission clause, termination of coverage, lapse of cover, surrender of the policy, three lines about premium payments, one line about renewal notice, one line about binding period. The expert keeps four (rescission, termination, lapse, surrender), drops the rest, and the dictionary entry for cancellation is built in five minutes of review.

Adjacent design-time cases with the same shape:

  • Cross-lingual dictionary bootstrap. A corpus contains contracts in French, correspondence in English, regulatory annexes in German. A multilingual embedding surfaces prime annuelle, annual premium, jährliche Prämie as top matches for each other. The expert validates once, the trio goes into the dictionary as aliases for the concept premium. From that point on, query-time retrieval runs on keywords in the language of the query with no embedding call.
  • Emotional lexicon for HR corpora. A HR analyst builds a distress-signal lexicon for employee-barometer verbatims. Seed feeling overwhelmed. Embedding surfaces drained, on the edge, hollow, running on empty, checked out. Analyst validates. The distress concept gets its dictionary entry.

The pattern is always the same. Discovery is offline. The result of discovery is a validated dictionary. The dictionary is what serves production. The embedding is scaffolding for the design team; it does not follow the product into runtime.

4. Ingestion time: keep the corpus clean

Every new document that enters the corpus is a moment where embeddings help without needing to run at every query. Two roles matter here.

Near-duplicate detection. In real corpora, the same document arrives more than once, and rarely in identical form:

  • Two versions of a contract, one scanned (OCR'd, some tokens corrupted), one re-typed cleanly by the legal team.
  • The same policy uploaded twice in the same folder because of a workflow glitch.
  • A regulatory circular republished in a slightly-edited version six months later.

Fingerprint hashes (MD5, SHA) do not catch these because a single OCR difference or a whitespace change breaks the hash. Line-level embedding does: two documents whose lines are 0.95 cosine on average are almost certainly the same document with minor variation. The ingestion pipeline flags them, and the human decides which version to index (usually the cleaner one, or the most recent).

Typical pattern in a broker's document workflow: the same insurance policy arrives twice, once as a scanned PDF (OCR-noisy) and once as a re-typed clean copy from the legal team. Fingerprint hashes see them as two distinct files. Line-level embedding sees the two versions as ~0.95 cosine on average, close enough to flag as duplicates. The ingestion pipeline keeps the cleaner version in the retrieval index and stores both in the archive for audit.

Semantic auto-tagging. As documents arrive, they need to be routed to the correct sub-corpus (contract vs claim vs correspondence vs policy statement). Some structural signals help (filename patterns, folder location, PDF metadata) but they are not always reliable. Embedding-based classification, using the concepts in the dictionary as anchors, gives a first-pass tag that the archivist confirms.

Concrete case. A document arrives without a clear filename. Embedding its first page against the dictionary concepts gives claim declaration at cosine 0.83, renewal notice at 0.42, contract clause at 0.31. The archivist confirms claim declaration, and the document is routed to the claims sub-corpus for indexing.

Both cases run once per document, then the result persists. The query-time retrieval never re-runs the embedding on the same document.

5. Query time: three ways embeddings help

Design time and ingestion time run offline; their embedding passes finish before any user asks anything. Query time is the phase where a vector call may need to run per query, and the constraints are strict: fully automated, hundreds of milliseconds, no human in the loop. Three specific patterns justify the vector pass at that phase. Everything else the pipeline should already have absorbed through the dictionary.

5.1 Typo and OCR fallback

Now we enter the phase where embeddings really do run at query time, but only for specific patterns that cannot be pre-validated.

Typos are unpredictable. A user types polciy renwal instead of policy renewal. A scanner turned déductible into déducctible in six pages of a French insurance PDF. There is no dictionary you can pre-build to cover every corruption. So the query-time loop has to handle it.

The loop:

  1. User query: polciy renwal.
  2. Keyword search on the dictionary: polciy matches no concept, renwal matches no concept. Zero pages returned.
  3. Fallback: embed the query at line level, search the corpus with cosine.
  4. The line-level embedding lands on pages that contain policy renewal, because sub-word tokenization absorbs the typos.
  5. The LLM sees the corpus lines, recognises policy and renewal as the intended terms, corrects the query internally.
  6. The corrected query re-runs against the dictionary, this time cleanly.
  7. Answer flows back with the correct citations.

Concrete case. A broker's assistant queries a 500-page scanned French insurance PDF for deductible for water damage. Half the corpus is OCR'd, and 40% of déductible occurrences got mangled into déducctible, déductibl., dèductible. Pure keyword search returns the un-corrupted 60% of pages. The embedding line-level fallback catches the rest, the LLM harmonises the corrupted spellings back to déductible, and the assistant gets 100% of the relevant pages instead of 60%.

This is the case where the vector pass is the only thing that works at query time. Keyword search cannot match a corrupted token. Sub-word tokenization absorbs the corruption. No dictionary maintenance covers every possible OCR artifact.

5.2 What the dictionary hasn't learned yet

Even on a well-maintained dictionary, the query-time reality is that some queries will hit a term the expert has not curated yet. Three sub-cases:

Fast-moving corpora. A retail bank launches a new savings product every quarter. Regulatory annexes updated monthly. Product names, campaign codes, promotional periods: the corpus vocabulary drifts faster than the dictionary review cycle. A user query on a two-week-old product misses the dictionary. Embedding fallback returns a plausible page rather than null.

Long-tail cross-lingual. Even after cross-lingual dictionary bootstrap (section 3), the tail is long. A French user asks about assurance dommages, and the English dictionary entry only has property insurance and damage coverage as aliases. The German equivalent Sachversicherung was never added because no German document was around during the initial design phase. Now a German document is in the corpus, and the query in French should find it. Multilingual embedding at query time catches the match; the surprise term goes into the expert's review queue for the next quarterly cadence.

Bootstrap phase. In the first weeks of a deployment, the dictionary is thin. Every query is a chance to discover the vocabulary the expert had not thought of. Embeddings serve as a graceful-degradation layer during bootstrap: they give the user something rather than null, while the observability layer captures which queries fell back so the expert can prioritise.

Concrete case. A newly-deployed RAG on a claims-management corpus, dictionary constructed on 20 sample claims. A real user queries subrogation waiver. The dictionary has subrogation but not waiver as an alias for renunciation. Keyword hits are weak. Embedding fallback finds three pages that discuss waiver of subrogation in a specific context. The user gets those pages. The query lands in the expert's queue, and next Monday waiver is added as an alias to renunciation.

Rule of thumb. If the queries falling back to embedding are more than roughly 15% of the total, the dictionary review cadence needs to accelerate. Embedding fallback is a safety net, not a substitute for dictionary maintenance.

5.3 Semantic ranking of keyword hits

The dictionary retrieves the pages that match the query's concept set. On a mature corpus, that can be twenty pages. The user needs the top three. How do you rank inside the keyword-matched set?

Naive ranking is by keyword count (more matches = higher rank). It works, but it is coarse. A page that contains deductible twenty times might just be a glossary or a summary page, while the page that answers the user's specific question about deductible for water damage contains the term twice, in the right context.

Embedding of the specific query, applied only over the keyword-filtered top-K, gives a contextual re-rank that takes the surrounding paragraph into account. It is fast (K ≈ 20 lines to score, not the whole corpus), auditable enough (the top of the funnel is still the keyword filter), and it lifts the answer-bearing line above the glossary.

Concrete case. Query deductible for water damage claims on a 200-page policy. Dictionary matches 24 pages that contain deductible. Embedding the specific query against those 24 pages ranks pages with water damage context on top, moving three pages of pure deductible boilerplate down. The user gets the right three pages first.

Contrast with pure vector search. If we skipped the dictionary and just embedded the query over the whole corpus, we would get the failure modes catalogued in Article 2A: topical proximity beats answer relevance, signal dilution, magnitude blindness. The keyword-filter-first-then-embedding-rank pattern gets the best of both: dictionary provides the audit trail and the recall guarantee, embedding provides the contextual sharpness within the recall set.

Practical note. This is not the same as a full reranker (cross-encoder). A cross-encoder is stronger but slower and heavier. Bi-encoder embedding for in-set ranking is usually enough at K ≈ 20 and preserves the low-latency profile of the pipeline.

6. The map: spine and connective tissue

The picture that emerges is not "keyword vs vector" and not "one is right, the other is wrong". It is keyword-first with embeddings supporting at every phase where the dictionary cannot fully anticipate.

The recap runs across the three phases:

  • Design time. The embedding surfaces aliases, cross-lingual pairs, and emotional vocabulary the expert did not have at the tip of their tongue. The expert validates; the result becomes the dictionary.
  • Ingestion time. The embedding catches near-duplicate documents that fingerprint hashes cannot see, and gives an auto-tagging first pass when the filename or metadata does not tell you the document's actual topic. The result persists in the index metadata.
  • Query time. Three specific roles justify the vector call per query. Typo and OCR fallback catches token corruption that keyword search cannot match. Long-tail / new-vocab fallback catches queries the dictionary has not learned yet, so the user gets something while the expert queue grows. Semantic ranking orders the keyword-filtered top-K by contextual relevance, lifting the answer-bearing line above the glossary.

The keyword dictionary carries the pipeline's audit trail, its speed, its cost profile, and its durability across model releases. Every one of those properties is what enterprise buyers want. Embeddings never replace that. They fill the gaps.

The reflex "embed everything" is too broad. The reflex "never embed anything" is too narrow. The right frame is: what is the dictionary supposed to do, and where does the dictionary hit its limit? That gap is where the embedding earns its slot.

7. Sources and further reading

The three-phase framework (design / ingestion / query time) is standard in enterprise search literature but rarely made explicit in RAG discussions, which tend to talk about "using embeddings" as one undifferentiated thing. Reimers and Gurevych (Sentence-BERT, 2019) remains the reference on line-level embedding, which underpins both the discovery loop and the query-time fallback described here. Karpukhin et al. (DPR, 2020) is the canonical dense beats BM25 result, worth reading against the article's frame, since DPR's context is in-domain open-domain QA where the training distribution matches the retrieval distribution, unlike enterprise corpora where the mismatch is exactly the problem embeddings alone cannot solve.

Same direction as the article:

  • Reimers & Gurevych, Sentence-BERT, EMNLP 2019 (arXiv:1908.10084). Line-level embedding as the natural granularity, underpinning discovery + fallback.
  • Formal et al., SPLADE, 2021 (arXiv:2107.05720). Learned sparse retrieval; a bridge between keyword and dense worlds, in the spirit of the spine + connective tissue frame.
  • Gao et al., HyDE: Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval, ACL 2023 (arXiv:2212.10496). The keywords-from-hypothetical-answer trick, another way to bridge user vocabulary and document vocabulary at query time.

Different angle, different context:

  • Karpukhin et al., Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain QA, EMNLP 2020 (arXiv:2004.04906). The dense beats BM25 result on open-domain QA; the article contrasts this with enterprise corpora where the training/deployment distribution gap is exactly what breaks pure dense retrieval.
  • Khattab & Zaharia, ColBERT, SIGIR 2020 (arXiv:2004.12832). Late-interaction retrieval as another way to preserve exact-token signal on top of dense representations, complementary to the keyword-first pattern.

The gap between what your keyword search alone returns and what the two passes combined return is the concrete size of what embeddings buy you on your specific corpus.
