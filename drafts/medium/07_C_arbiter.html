Hybrid search in most vector databases ends the same way. Run keyword and embedding retrieval, then merge the two ranked lists with Reciprocal Rank Fusion. RRF is the default in Pinecone, Weaviate, and Elastic, and it works on common cases without tuning. It also discards the one thing you need when a pick gets challenged: the reason each method returned the candidate in the first place.

Score fusion turns that reason into a rank index. A section title that matched the question word for word and a page that was merely close in vector space come out as the same number. Hand the same evidence to one LLM call as a structured brief and it ranks better, and it writes down why: this candidate is primary because the section heading matches, that one is discarded because the snippet reads like context, not an answer. On a top-10 pool the call runs in about a second, cheaper than embedding the whole document and far cheaper than a wrong answer that reaches a user. RRF still has a job as a pre-filter when the pool is too large to fit one call, top-200 first, then the LLM on the survivors. The ranking decision itself belongs to the LLM.

This article works through that final call, the arbiter, on the Attention Is All You Need paper. Section 1 builds the structured brief you hand the LLM and the four roles it assigns each candidate (primary, supporting, tangential, discarded). Section 2 explains why embeddings come last among the detectors, with a production ablation where deterministic dispatching beat an embedding-only baseline by 23 points. Section 3 covers how the pipeline says "not found" instead of guessing. Section 4 names the single typed object retrieval hands to generation.

This is the retrieval brick of Enterprise Document Intelligence, a series that builds an enterprise RAG system from four bricks: document parsing, question parsing, retrieval, and generation. It closes the retrieval brick, after the mental model of retrieval as filtering and the parallel anchor detectors that produced the candidates this call now ranks.

1. The LLM arbiter: the single LLM call at the end

This is the LLM call that Article 7B (anchor detection) placed at stage 3. That article produced candidates from each detector; this section is what happens to them. A candidate is a single passage a detector returned with its anchor (where it was matched), its aggregated unit (section, page, or chunk), and a snippet of surrounding context. The arbiter sees them all in one call, ranks them with reasons, and outputs the final list.

The three things this section covers:

  • Why score fusion (RRF and friends) loses the signal the detectors already made available.
  • What structured brief to hand the arbiter so it can rank with reasons.
  • What to record per candidate so any auditor can reconstruct the decision.

The pattern in one line: detectors propose, the arbiter decides, in a single call.

1.1 Score fusion is the wrong instinct

When multiple methods return candidates, the reflex is to combine their scores. The methods return numbers on different scales: cosine similarity in [0, 1], BM25 unbounded, co-occurrence integer counts. Adding them is meaningless. Normalizing each method's score to [0, 1] does not help either, because a 0.9 cosine and a 0.9 normalized BM25 do not mean the same thing about the same candidate.

The classical answer is Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF). It avoids the calibration problem by ignoring scores and using ranks:

RRF: rank-based fusion that ignores raw scores; k = 60

with k = 60 by convention. Candidates ranked by several methods accumulate contributions; candidates seen by only one method get a single small term. RRF is the default in many vector DBs (Pinecone, Weaviate, Elastic) and works on common cases without tuning.

But RRF misses real signal. Why a method ranked a candidate matters. TOC ranked it because a section title matched. Keywords ranked it because premium and $ co-occurred on the same line. Embeddings ranked it because something near the page was vaguely close in vector space. RRF compresses all of that to a rank index. The agreement across methods becomes a number, and the reason for the agreement disappears.

That is exactly what an expert reads on the screen: the section heading, the matched keywords, the lines around the match. We argue that a small LLM call, given the same information in a structured form, ranks better than any score-fusion method.

We still log RRF when the surrounding tool uses it by default, or use it as a cheap pre-filter when the candidate pool is too large to fit in an LLM call (top-200 by RRF, then LLM on the survivors). But the ranking decision belongs to the LLM, not to a score formula.

1.2 Hand the LLM a structured brief

The LLM is the layer that ranks. Its input is not "here are five passages, pick the best". It is a structured brief, one row per candidate, listing what each retrieval method found out:

  • candidate_id: stable reference (page + line range, or section + line offset).
  • methods: which retrieval methods showed this candidate (TOC, keywords, embeddings).
  • section: the TOC section the candidate sits in, lifted from toc_df.
  • matched_keywords: the keywords from the parsed question that landed on this candidate.
  • snippet: three to five lines of surrounding context, lifted from line_df.

The brief is what the LLM reads. It looks like what an expert sees on their screen: a section heading on top, the matched keywords in the body, the lines around the match. Much closer to that than to a ranked list of cosine scores. The LLM ranks the candidates and writes a one-line reason per kept candidate, which goes straight into the audit trail. Each candidate gets one of four roles:

  • primary: carries the answer.
  • supporting: gives context the answer needs to make sense.
  • tangential: related but kept low-priority.
  • discarded: the LLM's drop, with a reason recorded for audit.

The brief: section title, matched keywords, snippet; what an expert reads

llm_rank on this per-candidate brief is the standalone helper. When the document has a usable table of contents, the production default routes on the section instead (Section 1.4), because a page's snippet can be nothing but the running header. Keep this arbiter for content-rich candidates worth ranking one against another.

A minimal runnable arbiter. One LLM call, the whole candidate list at once, structured output back.

The whole arbiter in twenty lines: format briefs, ask one role + reason per candidate

Why this beats score fusion:

  • The LLM sees why each method ranked the candidate. A high-cosine match with no keyword overlap is probably topical noise. A TOC + keyword agreement is a real structural signal. RRF turns both into the same rank number.
  • The LLM can label candidates beyond keep/drop: primary, supporting, tangential. Useful when the answer has a main part and supporting context.
  • The LLM can flag contradictions: two passages saying different things on the same point. Common in contracts with amendments, in regulatory filings with revisions.
  • The justification is plain text and goes straight into the audit trail. No rrf_score = 0.0327 line to explain to compliance.

Cost: one LLM call (about a second for a top-10 candidate pool). Cheaper than running embeddings on the whole document. Much cheaper than a wrong answer in production.

1.3 Conflicts and the audit trail

When methods disagree, the LLM has to pick. Three rules of thumb help:

  • Trust the TOC on exact title matches. The author wrote that title. 3.5 Positional Encoding matched directly on the running question's keywords. No statistical method should override that.
  • Trust co-occurrence keywords with a strong signal. A line where primary and secondary keywords appear together (positional + sinusoidal, or positional + learned) is almost certainly relevant, even if it sits outside the TOC-narrowed scope.
  • Be skeptical of embedding-only hits: A candidate surfaced only by embeddings, with no TOC or keyword backing, needs a careful look. Sometimes a real synonym the keyword method missed. More often it is topical noise. The LLM, reading the brief, can usually tell which is which.

The structured brief is what lets the LLM apply those rules consistently. Score fusion compresses why each method ranked a candidate to a single rank index and discards those rules.

Every candidate must be defensible. Why was this passage shown to the user? The answer cannot be "the embedding similarity was 0.78". It has to be a clear chain: which methods showed this candidate, what brief was shown to the LLM, what role the LLM assigned, what reason it wrote. Each candidate carries that trail from retrieval through generation.

Every candidate carries the context the LLM saw plus the LLM's verdict

For each candidate that reaches the user, the system can answer: which methods voted for it, what was its rank in each, what did the LLM see, what role did the LLM assign and why, were any methods skipped and why.

A concrete entry for the page-5 candidate from the example above:

Each candidate carries methods, brief, role, reason; auditor never has to ask

This is not just for debugging. In compliance, legal, and audit contexts, this trail is the documentation that justifies the system's output. Without it, the retrieval pipeline is a black box, and a black box does not pass a regulatory review.

1.4 Sections, not page snippets

The brief above hands the LLM a snippet: three to five lines of context per candidate. That works when the candidate is a real passage. It breaks on a dense reference document, and the failure is worth seeing.

Ask the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 for every Subcategory under GOVERN. The answer sits on pages 21 and 22, in the Framework Core table. The top three lines of those pages are not the table. They are the running header, repeated on every page: NIST CSWP 29 / The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 / February 26, 2024. Two neighbouring pages hand the arbiter identical briefs: same section, no matched keywords, the same header snippet. Reading only that, the LLM cannot tell them apart, and it discards the very pages the TOC had found. The pipeline returned 12 of the 31 sub-codes, the rest filled in from memory.

The fix is to move the decision up a level. The production hybrid does not rank pages on snippets. It routes on the section. The keyword detector counts, per TOC section, how many of the question's keywords land in it, and hands that map to reason_on_toc (Article 7B). The router reads the outline plus those counts, picks the section, and the whole section is read, every page in its range. Keyword pages that fall outside the routed section stay as supporting context, capped. The LLM still decides in one call and the arbiter pattern still applies, but it decides on a section's title and keyword profile, a far more reliable brief than a page's top three lines. On the GOVERN question, that grounds all 31 sub-codes.

llm_rank stays useful as a standalone helper, for when the candidates are content-rich passages worth ranking one against another. It is no longer the default at the end of the hybrid: when the document has a usable table of contents, routing on the section is both cheaper and harder to fool.

2. Choosing the methods

2.1 Why embeddings come last

Most RAG tutorials, frameworks, and conference talks present embedding-based retrieval as the foundation of the system. The implicit assumption is that embeddings are the default; the only question is which embedding model to use.

After walking through the grid, the position of this series becomes concrete: embeddings occupy a supporting role on both tables (title embedding match on toc_df, chunk embedding match on line_df), neither of which is the recommended default for most enterprise documents. This is not a rejection of embeddings. It is a re-prioritization based on what works.

Reason 1, embeddings dilute high-signal tokens. Embed "Does section 10350 of the Insurance Code apply here?" and the vector is an average of "section", "insurance", "code", "apply", "10350". The token "10350" is the entire query, but it is one signal in seven. The vector for the right passage may not be the closest match. Content keyword match finds it instantly.

We saw a version of this in the minimal RAG pipeline with the famous epsilon-label-smoothing failure: the page that contained the answer was not even in the top 3 by cosine similarity. The page that won contained more of the visual style of the question, not its specific information content.

Reason 2, embeddings cannot distinguish related-but-different concepts. The vector for "premium" and the vector for "deductible" are close. Both are amounts in an insurance contract, both appear in similar contexts. A query for "premium" with cosine similarity returns passages mentioning "deductible" alongside passages mentioning "premium". If the embedding-based retrieval brings back primarily deductible passages, the LLM downstream has no way to disambiguate.

Same problem with negation, time references, and any semantic distinction that depends on small word changes. Embeddings blur these distinctions; that is both their strength and their weakness.

Reason 3, embeddings have no understanding of document structure. A vector database is a flat collection of chunks. It has no notion of "this chunk is in the Definitions section" or "this chunk is part of an annex". All of that structure was thrown away when the document was chunked.

For a question like "What does the warranty section say about flood damage?", an embedding search returns chunks that mention "warranty" and "flood", possibly from Definitions or Exclusions, neither of which is what the user wanted. TOC reasoning would have picked Warranty in one step.

Made concrete on the running paper. The user asks "what does Figure 2 show?". The question is dominated by one high-signal token (Figure 2); everything else is filler. Embedding averages all of it; regex anchors the exact token instantly.

Embedding dilutes the signal token; regex pulls every exact-match line in one pass

Embedding ranks pages by visual similarity to the query phrasing and may not put the actual Figure 2 mentions in the top-5 at all. The regex finds every line that contains the exact token, on the right pages, deterministically. One signal in seven, lost in the average; one signal lifted out by the right tool.

2.2 When embeddings win

Embeddings work well in three cases:

  • Vocabulary mismatch where rewrites help. "Exit early" embeds close to "early termination provisions" once the rewrites are in use. Title keyword match and content keyword match miss this; chunk embedding match catches it. (TOC reasoning also catches this for a different reason: the LLM understands that "exit early" relates to Termination through implication, not similarity.)
  • Conceptual or fuzzy questions: "Is the liability cap reasonable?": no specific keyword to search for. Embedding can expose passages with the right conceptual shape.
  • Documents without TOC and without specialized vocabulary. Memos, emails, blog posts. No structure to navigate; no expert dictionary to apply.

In all three cases, embeddings are not competing alone, they are combined with keyword and TOC methods (the hybrid embedding combination) for the structural awareness they lack.

In the wild: On one production system we measured the contribution of each retrieval method by ablating them one at a time, against a per-failure-mode evaluation:

- Embedding-only baseline: 71% accuracy.

- Adding TOC-based retrieval where applicable: 84%.

- Adding co-occurrence keyword retrieval with the expert dictionary: 91%.

- Adding the orchestrator to pick methods per question: 94%.

The 23-point gap between embeddings only and all methods with deterministic dispatching is what better retrieval buys. No embedding-model upgrade we tried came close.

HyDE is a special case of this principle. HyDE generates a hypothetical answer to the question, embeds it, then runs cosine similarity against the document chunks. The reason it works: the hypothetical answer carries the document's domain vocabulary that the question lacks. "How do I exit a contract early?" becomes "early termination, notice period, exit fees, written notice..." in the LLM's draft, which then matches the real passages in embedding space.

In this framework, the same logic is captured one stage earlier, at question parsing time, and codified into the expert keyword dictionary. HyDE is "embedding-based keyword expansion done implicitly per query"; this series favors "explicit keyword expansion done once and reused". Same payoff, with three operational gains:

  • the keyword set is auditable: the expert sees what matched and why
  • it pays its cost once instead of per query
  • it builds a permanent asset (the dictionary) instead of regenerating a one-shot hypothesis

In open-domain consumer settings without a domain expert and without auditability requirements, HyDE keeps a small advantage; in the enterprise contexts this series targets, explicit expansion is practically superior. The question parsing brick decomposes the mechanisms behind HyDE in detail and explains why the keyword-and-synonym one dominates when the document vocabulary is bounded.

2.3 The decision tree

How does the dispatcher decide which combination of cells to enable for a given question?

Deterministic decision tree: parsed question + document profile pick the methods

  1. If the document has a clean TOC, run title keyword match (always) and title embedding match (optional) as the toc-side detectors. The arbiter will use the TOC to reason about subtle title matches as part of its single call.
  2. On line_df, run content keyword match with the parsed question's keywords and co-occurrence boosts. This is the main detector; its hits feed the arbiter directly.
  3. Add embeddings on line_df in parallel when vocabulary mismatch is expected or when the question is conceptual. Optional, skippable when keyword signal is already clean.
  4. The LLM arbiter (Section 1) sees everything at once, ranks with reasons. This is the single LLM call that turns detector hits into a final list.
  5. On very large documents where the candidate pool is too big to fit one arbiter call, use reason-then-match as a pre-narrowing trick: one extra LLM call on toc_df picks the relevant sections, then content keyword runs inside, then the arbiter ranks the survivors. Two LLM calls total instead of one, only when scale demands it.

In production, most questions activate keyword + (optionally) embedding detectors and end with the arbiter. The strategy code below uses the older "toc_reasoning" / "reason_then_match" labels for backwards-compatibility; conceptually they map to "the arbiter does the reasoning" and "narrow before the arbiter" respectively. A follow-up integrated pipeline develops the orchestration around the dispatcher.

Common pitfall: Hard-coding the same retrieval strategy for all questions. A pipeline that always runs embedding retrieval, even for documents with a clean TOC and questions with specific keywords, is doing extra work that produces noisier candidates than the simpler methods would have. The dispatcher should choose; if it always chooses the same thing, you have removed the choice.

3. Saying "not found" reliably

A reliable system must be able to say no. This is harder than it sounds, because LLMs are trained to produce responses, not to withhold them.

The architecture has to support "not found" at every stage:

  • Metadata filtering returns zero documents. No retrieval is run. The system tells the user no document matches the scope.
  • TOC matching returns no relevant section. The pipeline falls through to the next method.
  • All retrieval methods return empty or low-confidence candidates. The pipeline reports "no answer found in the available documents," explaining what was searched.
  • Candidates exist but the LLM, in generation, cannot extract an answer. Handled at the generation stage, but the retrieval stage must pass forward enough metadata for generation to be conservative.

3.1 Why keywords prove absence, embeddings cannot

There is a deep asymmetry between retrieval methods on how they handle absence. Embedding retrieval always returns a top-k with continuous similarity scores. A page about premiums returns 0.78 for a question about exclusions; a page about exclusions returns 0.81. Where do you draw the line between found and not found? Nothing in the embedding space says "this is not the right answer," only "this is closer or farther than that other one." You can pick a threshold, but it is arbitrary; on a different document or a different question, the threshold breaks.

Keyword retrieval works the opposite way. If your expert dictionary for "exclusion" covers every form the topic can take in the corpus ("exclusion", "excluded", "not covered", "outside the scope", "does not cover"), and the document contains none of them, you have a defensible claim: the topic is not in this document. Same for amounts, dates, codes. When the regex for "\$\d+" matches nothing, there is nothing to match. The absence is evidence, not uncertainty.

This is exactly what a business user has always done with Ctrl+F. They search the term they expect, and when nothing comes up, they conclude the document does not cover it. The conclusion holds because they know which terms to look for. Thirty years of professional document work runs on this method. A retrieval pipeline that encodes the same expert vocabulary inherits the same property: zero hits across an exhaustive keyword set returns "not found" with a precise, auditable reason.

In enterprise settings (legal, compliance, finance), this is the difference between "I'm not sure" (hedging) and "none of these specific terms appear in the document, and they would be present if the answer were here" (a defensible search). The first is useless to a compliance officer. The second is the answer.

This argument is also why expert dictionaries (built at question parsing time) are worth the investment. A dictionary that covers a topic exhaustively is not just a way to find the answer when it exists; it is the mechanism that lets the system prove the answer does not exist when it does not.

How the dictionary gets exhaustive. That exhaustive dictionary is the goal, not the starting point, and embeddings are how it grows toward it. The first few times a question hits vocabulary the dictionary does not yet cover, keyword retrieval comes back empty and the embedding fallback runs. When it lands on the passage that answers the question, the discriminating token of that passage is the missing alias. A query for "prime" the dictionary cannot match returns, by similarity, a line about "cotisation"; the arbiter reading the brief confirms cotisation is the term this corpus uses for premium, and the alias joins the dictionary (Article 6's expert vocabulary). The next "prime" query is a keyword hit with no embedding call, and the system can now prove absence on the term it just learned. Embeddings turn from a retriever into a discovery tool: run a few times to mine the corpus vocabulary, then step back once the keyword set covers the vocabulary. Accumulating those confirmed aliases across sessions, through the app's feedback loop, is its own topic.

3.2 No answer beats a wrong one

In a consumer chatbot, a wrong answer is annoying. In an enterprise document QA system, it is expensive.

One example: a compliance officer asks whether a contract has a non-compete clause, the system returns "yes, 3 years" from the wrong passage, and the decision gets made in a meeting before anyone notices the clause was actually 1 year. A wrong answer in a confident tone gets acted on before anyone re-checks the source. No answer would have sent the officer back to the document. That is the trade: a confident wrong answer short-circuits verification and creates false certainty; not found keeps the user looking.

A retrieval pipeline that knows how to say "I didn't find anything" is more valuable than one with a higher recall rate but no humility.

4. The retrieval output: one JSON ready for generation

Everything in this article fed one purpose: produce the right candidates to hand to the generation stage. The methods, the LLM arbiter, the dispatcher, the "not found" handling, all of it converges on a single typed object.

This section names it explicitly. Retrieval delivers one RetrievalResult per (document, question) pair. The generation brick reads it, produces the answer, and never has to ask retrieval anything more. A follow-up orchestration loop can extend the same RetrievalResult if generation needs more context, but the contract shape stays the same.

4.1 One candidate, two scopes

Per the anchor-and-context framing of Article 7A (retrieval as filtering), every candidate carries both. The anchor is where the match landed: a narrow line range on a specific page, with the section it belongs to. The context is the extended window retrieval decided to pass forward: a paragraph, a section, or a window of N lines, again as a line range with page bounds.

Why both? Generation needs the anchor for citations (highlight the matched lines on the PDF, link the answer back to the precise lines) and the context for grounding (read the surrounding paragraph or section the answer sits in). Storing only the context loses the precise citation. Storing only the anchor loses the surrounding evidence that makes the answer interpretable.

The convention: the context's range always contains the anchor's range. The width of the context comes from question parsing, driven by the two orthogonal axes the parser tags: answer_shape (cardinality) and answer_type (value), plus answer_context on StructuralHints (how much surrounding text to read). A (single, amount) question with answer_context=line gets a narrow context (the anchor plus a few lines of safety margin). A (listing, text) question with answer_context=section gets the whole section.

4.2 The unified schema

One typed object per (document, question), ready for generation

The doc_id and question at the top are what makes the result replayable. Persist this object and you can re-run generation later, on the same retrieval, without paying for retrieval again. That is the basis for the audit trail (Section 1.3), for batch evaluation, and for any orchestrator that has to compare two generation prompts on the same retrieved evidence.

4.3 A live JSON on the running paper

The same question we used in Section 1 to demo the LLM arbiter: "What positional encoding does the paper use?". Two primary candidates survive, both anchored on the 3.5 Positional Encoding section, with contexts that extend to the full section body.

One JSON, two candidates with anchor + context, methods, keywords, arbiter verdict

When you build retrieval as a service or a batch step, this is the artifact that goes into the database, gets versioned, and supports replay. It is also what your auditor reads when they ask why a passage was shown to the user.

The same cache convention the parsing brick installs applies. save_retrieved_pages(pdf_path, question, retrieved_pages_df) writes the page-level result (with match_count, matched_keywords columns intact) to output///questions//retrieved_pages.xlsx. The filtered_line_df is intentionally not cached. Generation re-derives it from line_df + page numbers on the spot. Persisting page IDs is the cheap default; persisting the heavy filtered DataFrame would be duplicate state. This keeps each brick boundary clean: retrieval writes page selections, generation reads them, neither needs anything more.

5. Conclusion

Retrieval is not search. It is filtering on structured tables. Once parsing has produced line_df and toc_df, every retrieval method is a way of picking rows out of one or both. Anchor detection runs in three stages: keyword detection on line_df and toc_df (always, free) and optional embedding similarity in parallel, aggregation to a structural unit (section, page, chunk), then one LLM arbiter call that ranks the candidates with reasons. Keywords stay always-on because they are auditable and give a defensible "not found"; embeddings are optional and useful for vocabulary mismatch; BM25 underperforms business-coded keyword filtering on enterprise questions. Anchor small (line, title), expand for context (paragraph, section); collapsing the two granularities is the most common pipeline mistake.

Article 8 (generation) takes over from here, the fourth brick. With the right candidates retrieved at the right granularity, the LLM still has real work: extracting the answer, formatting it, citing it, refusing to invent when the answer is not there. That step has to run as controlled execution rather than free-form prediction.

Same direction as the article:

  • Robertson & Zaragoza, The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond, FnT IR 2009. Canonical BM25 reference; the article's claim that BM25 measures frequency where business retrieval needs co-occurrence rests on this.
  • Cormack, Clarke, Buettcher, Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet, SIGIR 2009. The fusion mechanic the article uses when combining keyword and embedding scorers.
  • Thakur et al., BEIR: A Heterogeneous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models, NeurIPS 2021 (arXiv:2104.08663). Empirical support for the article's claim that BM25 is a strong baseline that dense retrievers don't always beat.
  • Sarthi et al., RAPTOR: Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval, ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2401.18059). Hierarchical retrieval; the closest published equivalent to the TOC-as-retriever pattern. RAPTOR clusters the document into a tree; this article uses the TOC the document already declares.
  • Anthropic, Contextual Retrieval (Sept 2024 engineering post). Hybrid-search consensus that the article is in the same direction as.

Different angle, different context:

  • Karpukhin et al., Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering (DPR), EMNLP 2020 (arXiv:2004.04906). Dense retrieval as the production default. The context is in-domain open QA; this article puts BM25 / keywords first and embeddings last on enterprise corpora.
  • Khattab & Zaharia, ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT, SIGIR 2020 (arXiv:2004.12832). Token-level late interaction as the retrieval primitive. Different context from this article's anchor-vs-context separation.
  • Izacard et al., Unsupervised Dense Information Retrieval with Contrastive Learning (Contriever), 2022 (arXiv:2112.09118). Unsupervised dense retrieval reaching BM25-grade out-of-distribution performance. Useful contrast with the article's BM25-first stance on enterprise corpora.
  • Izacard et al., Atlas: Few-shot Learning with Retrieval Augmented Language Models, 2022 (arXiv:2208.03299). Large-scale dense retrieval + RAG study; useful context for the embedding-first family of approaches.

7. Run it on your own files

Want to wire the three-stage anchor detection into your own pipeline? The runnable notebook behind this article ships alongside a cleanly-structured Python package (typed, modular, one file per detector): match_titles and embed_match_titles for toc_df, co_occurrence_score and match_lexicon for line_df, the llm_rank arbiter for the single ranking call, plus the choose_methods dispatcher and the optional reason_then_match / section_weighted_match / hybrid_embedding combinations for large documents.
